Wednesday, August 5, 2026. Three companies levered to artificial intelligence reported after yesterday's close, and all three beat. AMD beat on revenue and earnings and guided the September quarter above the street. SpaceX beat consensus revenue by a billion dollars in its first disclosure as a public company and lost a quarter of what analysts had penciled in. Arista beat and then guided the next quarter roughly twelve percent above where the street had it. Two of those three stocks are lower this morning and one is up fourteen percent. The sorting principle is not growth, because AMD grew its data centre business 107 percent and is down nine. The sorting principle is cash. Companies that collect money from the buildout are being paid for it. Companies that spend money on the buildout are being charged for it, and as of this morning that charge applies even when their revenue is compounding at triple digits.

## Top Call: The Buildout Now Has a Payer and a Payee, and the Tape Only Wants to Own the Payee

Start with AMD, because the reaction looks wrong until you read the margin line. Revenue of $11.5 billion grew 50 percent year on year and 13 percent sequentially. Data centre revenue of $6.72 billion grew 107 percent and now accounts for 58 percent of the company. Adjusted earnings of $1.66 beat the $1.55 consensus. September quarter guidance of roughly $13 billion plus or minus $300 million implies 41 percent annual growth and another 13 percent sequentially. On any normal reading that is an excellent quarter from a company that has spent three years trying to prove it can be a genuine second source in accelerated compute. The stock is indicated down about nine percent near $481.

What broke it is that non-GAAP gross margin came in at 56 percent and was guided to roughly 56 percent again. AMD is now running something close to twenty points of gross margin below Nvidia, and every incremental dollar of Instinct revenue arrives at a mix that dilutes rather than levers the corporate margin. Investors will pay a very high multiple for growth that improves the margin structure. They will not pay that multiple for growth that merely holds it flat, particularly when the cash conversion is slowing behind it. Our read is that the demand question on AMD was answered decisively last night and the economics question was not. That is a materially better position than the company was in a year ago, but it is not what a stock priced for a trillion-dollar outcome needed to hear.

SpaceX is the cleaner illustration. Revenue of $7.8 billion grew 92 percent against a consensus near $6.8 billion. The net loss of $541 million compares with a consensus loss of $1.9 billion and with the $4.3 billion loss the company booked in the first quarter. On both lines this was one of the largest first-print beats the market has seen from a newly listed company of this size. The shares are down about eleven percent at $111.81, roughly seventeen percent below the $135 IPO price. The reason is a single number: capital expenditure of about $18.4 billion in the June quarter alone, which is more than twice the revenue the company generated in the same three months, driven by the artificial intelligence side of the business created by the February integration of xAI and X.

We wrote yesterday that a good print does not neutralise a supply event, and that the operating question and the share supply question should be kept separate. That is exactly what is playing out. The lock-up window opens tomorrow, August 6, releasing as much as twenty percent of restricted holdings and up to 911.5 million shares. Every holder considering selling into that window now has a headline capex figure to point at. The operating business inflected last night. The share count is about to. Those are two different facts and the second one is currently louder.

Then Arista. Revenue of $3.04 billion grew 38 percent against a $2.83 billion consensus, adjusted earnings of $1.02 beat the 88 cent estimate, and third-quarter guidance of roughly $3.3 billion in revenue and $1.06 to $1.08 in earnings sits far above the $2.95 billion and 92 cents the street carried. The stock is up around fourteen percent. Arista does not build data centres, does not buy accelerators, and does not carry the depreciation. It sells the switching fabric that connects other people's capital spending, and it is being valued accordingly.

The rule that emerges from these three prints is the same one that split Microsoft and Amazon from Meta last week, and the same one that lifted both Palantir and Caterpillar on Monday. Get paid by the buildout. Do not fund it. What is new this week is the severity with which the market is applying it to companies whose top lines are still doubling.

## Macro: A 44,000 Print Against a Four-Year High in Manufacturing

ADP reported private payrolls rose 44,000 in July against a 75,000 consensus, the slowest month since January, with June revised down to 95,000. Services added 47,000 and goods-producing industries lost 3,000. Education and health services contributed 36,000, which is to say that outside of the least cyclical category in the survey, private hiring in July was effectively zero.

Put that beside Monday's ISM Manufacturing at 55.6, a four-year high, and you have a spread that cannot persist. Manufacturing surveys at multi-year highs alongside shrinking goods payrolls is either a survey that is running hot on sentiment or a payroll series that is about to catch up. Our view is that the September policy case has quietly changed character. Twenty-four hours ago it was possible to argue for a hike on domestic demand strength. This morning that argument is thinner, and the September case rests almost entirely on inflation, where prices paid at 71.1 remains genuinely uncomfortable. Friday's July employment report, expected at 83,000 after 57,000, now carries more weight than it did yesterday.

Markets barely blinked at ADP, which we think understates the signal. The bond market did react: the ten-year is at 4.607 percent, the two-year is bid, and the thirty-year is easing back from a nineteen-year high. Swaps still put September at roughly 60 percent.

## The Gold Tell

The single most informative price on the screen this morning is gold, up close to three percent through $4,245 and briefly higher, with silver up more than three percent alongside it. That is not supposed to happen on the day Washington says it may sign an agreement to reopen the world's most important oil chokepoint. The textbook reaction to a war ending is gold lower and real yields higher.

Instead gold is ripping while the ten-year falls and the dollar sits flat at 99.85. That combination does not describe a geopolitical bid. It describes a rate bid layered on top of the debasement demand that has driven bullion all year, and it means the metal is trading the 44,000 payroll print rather than the Hormuz headline. When the equity tape and the gold tape disagree this sharply about the same morning's data, we would generally take the metal seriously. It is not a comfortable signal to hold alongside record index closes.

## Geopolitics: Priced, But Not Signed

President Trump said late Tuesday that a Hormuz agreement could come "tomorrow or the next day," adding that a lot of progress had been made and that talks were moving along very nicely. Treasury Secretary Bessent had said Tuesday that a deal could land today or tomorrow. Qatar confirmed an interim proposal has been drafted, and reporting indicates Washington, Tehran and Muscat are closing on an interim agreement with the United States aiming for an announcement today.

Crude's response tells you where positioning already sits. Brent lost more than ten percent across Monday and Tuesday, trading below $79 at one point, and this morning it is up 1.2 percent at $80.33 with WTI up 0.6 percent at $76.19. Oil is no longer falling on deal headlines because the deal is in the price. That creates an asymmetry worth naming plainly: if the agreement is signed today, the remaining downside in crude is modest, perhaps a few dollars. If the talks collapse, the move back up is not modest. Iran has continued to deny direct negotiations and describes the process as a discussion with Oman about a shipping route. We would not add to length in energy on a signature, and we would not be short volatility into a headline that has slipped twice already.

## Sector Spotlights

**Semiconductors and AI infrastructure.** The complex is not moving as one this morning and that is the point. Networking and memory are bid, accelerator vendors are not, and the differentiator is where each company sits relative to the capital spending. Arista up fourteen percent and AMD down nine on the same night is a distinction the market was not making three months ago.

**Healthcare.** Lilly, Novo and CVS in one session produced the widest dispersion of the quarter. Lilly raised full-year revenue guidance by three billion dollars, Novo guided to a full-year sales decline while restructuring, and CVS raised its profit outlook. Obesity remains the single largest earnings swing factor in the sector and it is now clearly a one-company story on the growth side.

**Energy.** Crude bouncing 1.2 percent on the morning a Hormuz agreement is supposed to be announced tells you the trade is complete. After a ten percent two-day decline, the risk in energy has flipped from headline risk to positioning risk. Refiners and shippers are the more interesting expression of a reopening than the majors.

**Media and consumer.** Disney's streaming profitability and the TikTok deal sit against Uber's revenue shortfall and McDonald's soft comparable sales from Monday. The consumer datapoints continue to look weaker than the index level, and the ADP print reinforces that read rather than contradicting it.
