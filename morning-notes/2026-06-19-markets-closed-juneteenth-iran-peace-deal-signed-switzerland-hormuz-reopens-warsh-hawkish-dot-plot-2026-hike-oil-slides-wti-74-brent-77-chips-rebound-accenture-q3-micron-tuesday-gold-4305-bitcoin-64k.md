Friday, June 19, 2026. US equity and bond markets are closed today for Juneteenth and reopen Monday, June 22, so there is no live domestic tape and no futures print to react to. The session is even quieter across Asia, where Hong Kong is dark for Tuen Ng and mainland China is shut for a three-day Dragon Boat holiday that runs through Sunday. Japan is the one major market open, with the Nikkei 225 grinding higher again and brushing 72,000 intraday. The headline event sits outside the trading day entirely: the United States and Iran formally sign their peace agreement in Switzerland this afternoon, sealing the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz to toll-free commercial shipping and closing the book on a conflict that has driven the macro narrative since late February. With no US prices moving, this note is best read as a week-in-review and a setup for Monday, when the market reopens to digest both the deal and the harder reality of Wednesday's hawkish Fed.

## Top Call: A Quiet Holiday Caps a Loud Week

The week that just closed was defined by two opposing forces, and both matter for how Monday opens. First, Kevin Warsh used his FOMC debut to deliver a genuine hawkish surprise. The committee held at 3.50 to 3.75 percent, but the dot plot flipped from an implied cut to an implied hike, with nine of eighteen officials now penciling in a 2026 increase and the median dot lifted to 3.8 percent. Second, the geopolitical risk that had hung over the tape all spring is being formally retired today with the Iran signing. Thursday's chip-led rebound, which clawed back almost everything the Fed-day selloff took out, tells you the market wants to look through the hawkish dots and lean into the peace dividend of cheaper oil and one less tail risk. We think that instinct is roughly right but premature. Higher-for-longer is now the Fed's stated bias, not a market fear, and the back half of June brings Micron on Tuesday as the next real test of the AI-memory thesis. Positioning into the long weekend: constructive on risk, respectful of the bond market, and watching whether the 10-year holds below 4.50 percent.

## The Fed: Warsh's Hawkish Debut Reframes the Year

Wednesday was the pivot of the week. The rate hold was never in doubt, but the surrounding communication was the most hawkish surprise in years. The Summary of Economic Projections moved the median 2026 dot to 3.8 percent from 3.4 percent in March, which means the central tendency now points to one more hike rather than the cut the market had been carrying. Nine of eighteen participants see at least one increase by year-end, and seventeen of eighteen judged the risks to inflation as tilted to the upside. Just as important, the statement stripped out its prior easing language and dropped references to future adjustments, and Warsh used his first press conference to lean away from forward guidance altogether in favor of a purely data-dependent stance.

The market reaction was a textbook two-day round trip. Stocks sold off Wednesday afternoon, with the S&P down 0.6 percent and the Nasdaq off 0.7 percent into the close, before Thursday's sharp rebound led by semiconductors carried the S&P up 1.08 percent to 7,500.58 and the Nasdaq Composite up 1.91 percent to 26,517.93. The cleaner tell is in rates and the dollar, where the move has stuck: the two-year yield jumped on the hawkish dots, the 10-year sits near 4.46 percent, and the dollar index pushed back above 100. That is the more honest read on the meeting. Equity investors are willing to celebrate disinflating oil and a finished war, but the rates complex is pricing a Fed that has quietly removed the cut it was supposed to deliver. If May CPI at 4.2 percent and PPI at 6.5 percent do not cool over the summer, the gap between a risk-on equity tape and a higher-for-longer curve becomes the central tension of the third quarter.

## Geopolitics: The Iran Deal Gets Signed

The defining headline of the day is the formal signing of the US-Iran peace agreement in Switzerland. The memorandum of understanding agreed earlier in the week immediately ends hostilities and reopens the Strait of Hormuz to toll-free commercial shipping, with vessels already beginning to transit after weeks of disruption. The agreement also sets the stage for broader direct negotiations between Washington and Tehran on the harder issues, including the nuclear program, which were deliberately carved out so the ceasefire and the shipping lane could be locked in first.

For markets the significance is almost entirely in the energy channel. The Strait carries roughly a fifth of seaborne oil and LNG, and its closure since the late-February escalation was the single biggest driver of the spring price spike. Reopening it removes the war premium that had inflated crude, and that is precisely what the oil tape has been pricing all week. The residual risks are about execution rather than direction. Mine-clearing and the repositioning of tankers will take time, hardliner opposition inside Tehran remains a wildcard, and the timeline for sanctions relief is still vague. But the broad direction is set, and the reduction in geopolitical tail risk is unambiguously positive for energy importers, consumer-facing sectors, and overall risk appetite heading into the second half.

## Oil: The Slide Has Legs

Crude extended its decline into the close of the week, with Brent slipping below $78 and WTI trading near $74, the lowest levels since early March. Oil has now fallen roughly 38 percent from its April peak, a remarkable unwind of the entire war premium in a matter of weeks. The drivers are doubly bearish. On the geopolitical side, the Hormuz reopening restores blocked supply just as the conflict that justified the premium ends. On the fundamental side, OPEC has been adding barrels and cutting its own demand outlook at the same time, trimming its 2026 global demand growth forecast again in its latest monthly report.

The cross-asset read matters more than the headline price. Falling energy costs are a direct disinflationary force and a tailwind for the consumer, which is exactly the kind of help the economy needs as the Fed signals it would rather hold or hike than cut. The tension is that the same cheaper oil that supports households also drains the energy complex, which has been the weakest corner of the equity market all month as the war bid evaporates. For now the consumer benefit outweighs the sector drag, but a full Hormuz normalization that tips the global balance into surplus would keep pressure on energy equities well into the summer.

## Corporate: Accenture Headlines a Thin Calendar

With the holiday bracketing the week, the earnings flow was light, but Accenture's Thursday print was the one worth reading for an AI-services pulse. The results were solid on the surface and softer underneath. Revenue rose to $18.7 billion, up 6 percent in dollars, with operating margin at 17 percent and EPS up 9 percent to $3.80. The blemish was bookings, which fell 2 percent in dollars to $19.3 billion, the line investors care most about because it sets next year's revenue. The offset is the large-deal momentum, with 104 contracts of $100 million or more year-to-date, up 13 percent, increasingly tied to large-scale AI transformation programs. The read-through is that enterprise AI spending is real and concentrated in big platform overhauls, but the broader consulting cycle is still choppy. We read it as a mild positive for the AI infrastructure names and a neutral-to-cautious signal for the breadth of corporate IT budgets.

Earlier in the week Jabil delivered a clean beat, with quarterly revenue of $8.8 billion and EPS of $2.59, a useful confirmation that the hardware and electronics supply chain feeding AI buildouts remains busy. On the deal front, the M&A engine kept running through the holiday week: Olin agreed to buy Huntsman for about $4.3 billion, Salesforce announced a roughly $3.6 billion purchase of Fin to bolster its agentic AI stack, and Yum Brands agreed to sell Pizza Hut in two deals worth $2.7 billion combined. None of these reset the tape, but the steady flow of strategic activity is consistent with a financing backdrop that is still open despite the hawkish Fed.

## Semiconductors: Micron Is the Real Test on Tuesday

The chip complex did the heavy lifting in Thursday's rebound, and the group now walks into the most important print of the month. Micron reports fiscal Q3 on Tuesday, June 24, and expectations are stretched in both directions. The Street is modeling roughly $34.7 billion in revenue, up about 272 percent year-over-year, and EPS near $19.95, with the stock already up around 246 percent on the year on the high-bandwidth-memory supercycle that feeds AI accelerators. The single cleanest number to watch is gross margin, guided near 81 percent, because that is the most direct measure of HBM pricing power and the truest read on whether the cycle is still climbing or beginning to crest.

The bull case is structural. Micron says its entire 2026 HBM output is sold out under multi-year contracts, an important departure from the spot-driven boom-bust pattern of past memory cycles. The bear case is the lingering shadow of Broadcom's early-June guidance scare, which rattled the whole complex by raising questions about the linearity of hyperscaler spending, and a memory market that is straining as producers divert capacity to data-center customers at the expense of phones and PCs. After Micron, the demand signal for the AI trade will be a lot less abstract. A clean beat with margins holding would validate the Thursday bounce and likely pull the rest of the group higher into quarter-end. Any wobble on margins or 2027 commentary would land hard on a sector that has already priced a lot of good news.

## Asia and Europe Overnight

Greater China is offline. Hong Kong is closed for Tuen Ng and mainland exchanges are shut for the Dragon Boat holiday through the weekend, which thins regional liquidity considerably. Japan carried the load, with the Nikkei 225 adding about half a percent and trading as high as 71,952, extending a run powered by a weak yen, a 17 percent surge in May exports, and global demand for semiconductor equipment. The one caution flag in Tokyo is the Bank of Japan's recent move to 1 percent, the highest in three decades, which incrementally erodes the carry trade that has fueled Japanese equity inflows. The index keeps hitting resistance just above 70,000 even as it makes new highs, a sign the easy part of the move may be behind it.

Europe is open and trading without a US lead, drifting modestly higher in sympathy with Wall Street's Thursday rebound and lower energy prices. The Stoxx 600 and the DAX are both up around a quarter to a third of a percent in quiet conditions, with cheaper crude helping the consumer and industrial complex while energy names lag. With New York shut, volumes are light and the price action carries little signal into Monday.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran:** Formal signing today in Switzerland. Ceasefire holds and the Strait of Hormuz reopens to toll-free shipping, with tankers already transiting. Nuclear and sanctions issues deferred to follow-on direct talks. Execution risk is in mine-clearing, tanker repositioning, and hardliner pushback in Tehran, not in the direction of the deal.

**Ukraine-Russia:** The conflict remains overshadowed by the Iran resolution but is structurally unresolved. Daily combat continues along the Pokrovsk and Huliaipole axes and ceasefire talks are stalled. A pending US security-assistance package remains the item to watch for headline risk.

**Taiwan Strait and South China Sea:** PRC coast guard pressure near the Pratas Islands continues at a low simmer with no major escalation. The pattern of incremental Chinese activity persists but stays contained for now.
