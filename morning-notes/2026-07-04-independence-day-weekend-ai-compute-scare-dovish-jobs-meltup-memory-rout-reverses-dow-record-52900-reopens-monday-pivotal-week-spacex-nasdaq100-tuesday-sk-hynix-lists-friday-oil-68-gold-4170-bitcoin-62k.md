Saturday, July 4, 2026, and this is a holiday weekend note. US cash and bond markets have been shut since Thursday's close, closed Friday for Independence Day observed and dark again today for the Fourth itself, so the next real print is Monday, July 6. That leaves a two-session gap to think, and it lands at a useful moment, because the week that just ended packed a full cycle of fear and relief into four trading days. An AI-compute scare that started with Meta and rippled through the entire memory complex collided with the most dovish jobs report in months, and by the time Asia and Europe closed Friday the panic had almost entirely unwound. The Dow sits at a record, the memory names that looked broken on Thursday are ripping again, and Wall Street reopens into one of the busiest setups of the year. This note is less about a single overnight tape and more about what to carry into that week.

## Top Call: The AI-Demand Scare Was Real, the Memory Crash Was Not

The one thing to hold over the long weekend is that this week produced a genuine new worry and a fake one, and it is worth keeping them apart. The genuine worry is Meta Compute. When the largest AI spender in the world announces it will rent out excess capacity, it tells you the supply of compute is starting to catch up with demand, and that is a legitimate second-half question for every name levered to AI buildout. The fake worry was the idea that this breaks the memory thesis. A group that round-tripped a double-digit two-day crash in a single Friday session, right after Micron printed a blowout quarter, was never trading on fundamentals on the way down. Our read is that the correct posture into Monday is constructive but selective. We would rather own pricing power that is scarce, memory and high-end storage, than raw compute that Meta is now signaling could become a commodity. The tape spent the week rotating out of the crowded winners and into laggards, defensives, and small caps, and a dovish Fed only strengthens that rotation. This is a market to lean into on breadth, not to chase on the mega-cap names everyone already owns.

## The Week in Review: A Full Fear-and-Relief Cycle in Four Sessions

The story of the week was compression. What might normally play out over a month happened in four sessions. It opened with Meta popping on the compute news while the chips it competes for sold off, an early sign that the AI trade was starting to separate the toll-takers from the toll-payers. By midweek the selling had metastasized into a full memory rout, with Micron down more than 10 percent even as it reported record fiscal results, and AMD and Intel falling in sympathy. Thursday exported the panic to Asia, where two names make up roughly half the Kospi, so a memory selloff became a Korean market selloff and Seoul fell close to 8 percent. Then the June jobs report landed soft, the Fed-hike narrative flipped toward cuts, and the mood turned on a dime. By Friday the memory complex had reversed almost the entire move, the Kospi rebounded 5 percent, and Europe printed a record. The lesson for positioning is that the fundamental driver, cooling labor and a dovish Fed, never wavered all week. The violence was all positioning, and positioning that unwinds this fast tends to leave the underlying trend intact.

## Macro: A Dovish Jobs Miss Reset the Whole Rate Path

The number that framed everything was June payrolls. The economy added just 57,000 jobs against a consensus near 113,000, the third soft labor print in a row after ADP came in at 98,000, and the unemployment rate ticked to 4.2 percent. The market read it cleanly. A labor market cooling this quickly pulls the Fed off the hike path it had been flirting with, and the odds of a July hike fell to roughly 20 percent from 30 percent a week earlier, with a September cut now back on the table as a live possibility. That single repricing explains most of the week's cross-asset moves. It is why gold rebounded 2 percent off an eight-month low to press $4,170, why the dollar softened, why small caps outperformed, and why global equities from Frankfurt to Seoul caught a bid. The tension that keeps us from turning fully offensive is that new Chair Kevin Warsh has spent recent weeks insisting inflation is still too high, and a bloc of his colleagues has floated hikes rather than cuts. A labor market printing under 60,000 jobs while the central bank frets about prices is a stagflation-lite setup, and it argues for owning the rate-cut beneficiaries without paying up for long-duration growth on faith. With the bond market closed, the two-year sits frozen near 4.02 percent and the ten-year near 4.36 percent until Monday.

## The Week Ahead: One of the Loaded Ones

The reason this weekend matters more than most is what sits on the other side of it. Tuesday, July 7, SpaceX officially joins the Nasdaq-100, and every index fund and ETF tracking the NDX, more than 200 products against upward of $800 billion, has to buy the stock into a limited float. That is mechanical, price-insensitive demand into a name that just upsized its debut investment-grade bond to $25 billion to fund its AI ambitions and stacked roughly $26 billion in annualized AI infrastructure revenue through deals with Alphabet, Anthropic, and a fresh $6.3 billion contract with Reflection AI. Then Friday, July 10, SK Hynix debuts its roughly $29 billion Nasdaq listing, dropping a new mega-cap memory name into a market that just spent a week fighting over exactly this group. Between those two events, capital will get pulled around inside the AI complex, and a passive bid for SpaceX plus a fresh supply of SK Hynix paper can distort the tape well beyond what the fundamentals justify. Layer in the start of Q2 earnings season, with the big banks reporting from July 14, and the quiet of this holiday weekend is the last still moment before a genuinely busy stretch.

## Sector Spotlight: AI Infrastructure Splits Into Toll-Takers and Toll-Payers

The most important structural shift this week was not a price move, it was a reframing. For two years the AI trade has been a scarcity story, where anyone who owned compute or the memory that feeds it held a scarce asset with pricing power. Meta Compute, alongside a similar move by SpaceX to monetize spare capacity, starts to turn that scarcity into supply. The read-through is a widening split inside the complex. The hyperscalers that overbuilt, Meta chief among them at as much as $145 billion of capex this year, get a new revenue line and a way to defend returns on their spending, which is why Meta popped on its own news. The names most exposed are the merchant compute providers whose margins depend on GPU time staying scarce. The memory makers sit in a more comfortable middle. Their product is genuinely supply-constrained, high-bandwidth memory cannot be spun up overnight the way a cloud rack can be re-rented, which is why Micron's pricing power held even through the panic. Our take is that the trade from here is quality of scarcity. We want the parts of the stack that cannot be commoditized by a hyperscaler flipping on excess capacity, and we would be more cautious on the parts that can.

## Sector Spotlight: Energy Stays Soft on a Supply Glut

Crude drifted lower into the holiday, with WTI settling near $68 and Brent near $71, both down sharply over the past month. The structural story is unchanged and it is bearish. Iran says it has moved tens of millions of barrels since the naval blockade came off, Russian exports are running at record levels, OPEC keeps adding supply, and the market has stopped pricing any real risk of a Hormuz closure. For portfolios this is clean disinflation that reinforces the rate-cut case a soft jobs report just strengthened. The nuance worth holding is the one that has run under the surface all week. Oil this weak is partly a peace-and-supply story and partly a demand story, and a market cheering the disinflation should keep asking whether crude is also whispering that global growth is cooling faster than a record-adjacent Dow assumes.

## Sector Spotlight: Financials and Defensives Led the Rotation

Underneath the AI drama, the leadership change is the real signal. On the day the Dow printed a record, it did so on financials and defensives, not tech, while the Nasdaq fell. Europe told the same story, with money rotating into healthcare, financials, and defence as the Stoxx 600 hit an all-time high. This is what a dovish-Fed, cooling-growth tape rewards, and it is why the banks kicking off earnings next week matter beyond their own numbers. If the rotation into value and rate-sensitives has legs, the banks are the first clean read on whether the broadening is fundamental or just a positioning rebalance. We are watching net interest margin guidance and credit commentary closely, because a labor market softening this fast eventually shows up in loan books.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran:** The indirect technical talks in Doha concluded with reported progress but no breakthrough, with delegations working the Strait of Hormuz and Iran's frozen assets. Shipping is operationally normal, with more than 30 verified transits in the latest daily count, though international shipping unions still designate the Strait a warlike operations area. The unresolved fight is over transit tolls rather than missiles, and the June memorandum remains a framework rather than a signature. This is still the single largest swing factor for oil and gold.

**Ukraine-Russia:** A meaningful shift this week. The fall of Viktor Orban in Hungary removed the last obstacle to a roughly 90 billion euro EU loan to Kyiv covering urgent budget and defense-industrial needs through 2027. Russia has escalated air raids on major Ukrainian cities, partly to distract from Ukrainian long-range strikes aimed at Russian oil and weapons production. Those strikes are a live tail risk for the oil glut narrative if they start to bite Russian output.

**Taiwan Strait and South China Sea:** PRC coast guard pressure near the Pratas Islands continues at a low simmer with no major escalation. The pattern of incremental activity persists but stays contained.
