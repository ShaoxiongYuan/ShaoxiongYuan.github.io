Monday, August 24, 2026, written at 9:15 a.m. Eastern, roughly fifteen minutes before the cash open, so every US level below is a pre-market mark and the two o'clock sanctions headline has not printed yet. Asia closed hard, Europe is flat with technology leaking, and the single most informative event of the weekend was not a policy decision but a capital raise.

## Top Call: The AI Buildout Now Runs on External Capital, and It Just Got Priced

Alibaba priced HK$80 billion, about $10.2 billion, of new stock over the weekend: 710 million shares at HK$112.70, closing Wednesday, with one hundred percent of net proceeds earmarked for full-stack AI capability and infrastructure. It is the largest primary follow-on in the history of the Hong Kong market and the third largest anywhere this year behind Alphabet and Intel. The book was covered close to three times. The Hong Kong line still fell about 9.5% and traded as much as 10.5% lower to a one-month low.

Put that next to Friday's other headline. Broadcom is seeking more than $60 billion of debt in a package that could reach $100 billion, with a $30 billion junior tranche and Blackstone and Apollo at the table, to fund Anthropic's custom silicon. Inside three sessions, two of the largest builders of AI compute have gone to the external capital markets rather than to operating cash flow, one in equity and one in leverage. That is the fact that matters, and it is a change in kind rather than in degree.

Our read is that the market has finally been handed a price for the marginal dollar of AI capital expenditure, and the price is roughly ten percent of the buyer's equity. For eighteen months the complex has been valued as though incremental compute spending was self-funding and therefore free at the corporate level. A three-times-covered book that still trades down ten percent says holders will fund the buildout but will no longer do it at par. This is the same trade that took semiconductors down about five percent last week while gold rose almost five percent, and it is the reason we treat this morning's technology weakness as continuation rather than a new shock.

## Iran: Economic D-Day at Two O'Clock, and Crude Is Falling Into It

Scott Bessent holds his press conference at 2:00 p.m. Eastern to detail what he called, in a Financial Times op-ed published ahead of it, "an economic D-Day, the single greatest financial offensive ever marshalled against an adversary." The design is already public in outline: the campaign targets Iran's trading counterparties rather than Iran alone. What is not public is the part that determines the market impact, namely the specific penalties, the named entities, the exemptions and the enforcement dates.

The reason this is a China story rather than an Iran story is arithmetic. On 2025 Kpler cargo-tracking data, China buys more than eighty percent of Iran's seaborne crude. A secondary sanctions regime built to shut those exports has to reach Chinese refiners, Chinese banks or Chinese ports to work at all. That converts an Iran policy into a China policy about a month before the US-China summit scheduled for late September.

Tehran answered over the weekend, and answered specifically. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, said that if the countries around Iran join the American economic campaign, "not a drop of oil will leave the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz," and he extended the threat to the alternative pipeline routes that have been carrying barrels around the strait. Six months into the conflict, with no strikes between the parties for several weeks and no meaningful talks either, that is the widest threat Iran has issued.

And crude is down. WTI is $85.69, off 1.57%, and Brent is around $92 after a second consecutive weekly gain of roughly six percent. This is the morning's cleanest analytical puzzle, because a sanctions regime aimed at the buyers of a producer's oil is mechanically bullish for everyone else's oil, and the producer has just threatened to close the waterway. There are only two readings that fit. Either the market expects the package to arrive with enough carve-outs and delayed effective dates that near-term barrels are unaffected, or positioning had already run the premium up over two weeks and this is profit-taking ahead of a scheduled event. We think it is mostly the second with a real dose of the first, and we would rather be long the risk than short it into a 2:00 p.m. text nobody has read. European trading agreed with the price rather than the risk this morning, with travel and leisure the best sector at plus one percent on cheaper fuel and energy the worst at minus 0.6%.

## Korea: Samsung Gives the Whole Rally Back, and We Were on the Wrong Side of It

The Kospi closed at 6,696.96, down 215.99 points or 3.12%, with Samsung Electronics off 8.70% and SK Hynix down 3.41%. Foreign investors sold a net 3.69 trillion won and institutions another 1.29 trillion. The cause was the shareholder return package, which Samsung set at up to 110 trillion won, about $79.6 billion, and which analysts judged smaller than the market had built in.

We should own this directly. Friday's note recorded Samsung rising 3.87% on reports of a ninety to a hundred and ten trillion won package and used Korea's strength as evidence that last week's US semiconductor selling was positioning rather than a change in the memory cycle. The package landed at the top of the reported range and the stock fell almost nine percent. The specific inference was wrong, and the reason is instructive: when a stock rallies on the report of a number and then falls on the confirmation of that same number, the rally was pricing the upside tail rather than the range. That is a warning worth carrying into Wednesday night, because Nvidia arrives with a similar setup.

What we would not concede is the underlying claim. SK Hynix fell a third as much as Samsung on a day driven by Samsung's own capital allocation, and nothing in the session touched pricing, bookings or the 2027 supply position. This was a capital return disappointment inside a single issuer, not a memory demand signal.

## The Trade War Nobody Priced

United States and Canada trade talks collapsed late Friday after nearly two weeks of negotiation. Washington imposed fifty percent tariffs on a set of Canadian products on Saturday, covering building materials, liquor, certain clothing and, in the detail that will get quoted for a year, hockey sticks. Canada retaliates on September 8, targeting steel, dairy, agricultural equipment and pulp and paper. Mark Carney said the United States made a last-minute power play by trying to constrain Canada's ability to negotiate with third countries, and added demands late on autos, Canadian culture and French-language protections. He has called the tariffs a miscalculation and described his country as being at war with its neighbour. Officials on both sides expect no new talks before the US midterms.

The market response is essentially nothing: Dow futures are down thirteen basis points. That may be correct on the direct arithmetic, since the affected product lines are narrow. It is harder to defend on the second-order reading. Ten days ago Canada was removing the majority of its retaliatory tariffs, and we treated that as the one constructive trade development of the week. It has reversed completely, and it reversed because of demands about sovereignty rather than about trade balances, which is exactly the sort of dispute that does not resolve on a spreadsheet. A fifty percent tariff on a G7 neighbour with a two-week fuse and no negotiating channel before November is not priced anywhere we can find.

**Our take on Nvidia**: the setup has improved and the bar has moved the wrong way at the same time. Consensus wants roughly ninety-seven percent revenue growth, the buy side is clustered nearer $93 to $95 billion against the company's own $91 billion guide, and the stock has fallen after the print in six of the last eight quarters. That last statistic is the Samsung lesson in a different jersey: a beat against the published number is not the same as a beat against what is actually positioned. The offset is that the sector has already given back about five percent, so the entry is materially better than it was in May. We would still rather own the print than fade it, and we would rather express it in Nvidia than in the ASIC names, because a Marvell that has to clear a thirty-five percent growth bar two days later carries the harder comparison.

The other name we are watching without owning is Alibaba itself. A ten percent markdown on a three-times-covered book, with proceeds fully committed to AI infrastructure rather than to a balance sheet repair, is the market questioning payback rather than solvency. If that question stays open, it prices every hyperscaler's next funding round, not just this one.

## Positioning

Three views into the open. First, the AI complex is being repriced on funding cost rather than on demand, and Alibaba plus Broadcom inside three sessions is the evidence; we stay constructive on the demand names and cautious on anyone who has to issue to keep spending. Second, crude into a two o'clock sanctions text with Iran threatening the entire Gulf is the wrong thing to be short, and we treat this morning's 1.57% decline as an entry rather than a signal. Third, the debasement trade has not blinked, with gold at $4,645.74 and Bitcoin at $79,043 while the long end eased and the dollar stayed under ninety-nine, and until the two-year confirms it we continue to size that expression in the metals rather than the currency.
