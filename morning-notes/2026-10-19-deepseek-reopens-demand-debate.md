Monday, October 19, 2026, written at 9:15 a.m. Eastern, fifteen minutes before the open. Every future, yield, currency and commodity level in this note carries that stamp, and the European figures are intraday rather than settled. Hong Kong was shut for the Chung Yeung Festival. The Federal Reserve is in its quiet period until October 28. Netflix reports tomorrow after the close, and Tesla, Meta and IBM all report Wednesday, so the week's real information arrives in the back half.

## Top Call: The Bear Case Just Acquired a Second Leg

DeepSeek released V4.1-Flash over the weekend with a claim about architecture rather than capability. The model is said to need roughly 75% less high bandwidth memory and 87.5% less solid state storage than its predecessor to run. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix fell more than 3% in Seoul on it, and a Bloomberg gauge of Asian chipmakers hit its lowest level since July.

For nine sessions this desk has argued that the demand debate in artificial intelligence was settled and that the live question was funding. That framing is now incomplete, and we would rather say so on the morning it breaks than three sessions later. An efficiency claim of this size, if it survives contact with independent testing, does not reduce the appetite for intelligence. It reduces the quantity of memory required to deliver a unit of it, and memory is the one part of this complex where pricing power was being underwritten through 2027 as a structural fact.

So the market now carries two bear cases that are independent of each other. Credit says the assets cost more to finance than the plan assumed. Efficiency says you may need fewer of the assets. Either one on its own is survivable, and the market has been pricing the first one for two weeks without breaking. Together they compound, because the thing that makes an expensive balance sheet bearable is volume growth, and the thing that makes a volume forecast bearable is cheap money. Remove both and you are left with multiple compression in the most crowded industry group on the tape.

We would hold one caution against our own alarm. This is a vendor claim about a vendor model, with no independent benchmark yet and no disclosure of what the efficiency costs in accuracy or latency. The January 2025 precedent is instructive in both directions: the original DeepSeek shock was real information about the cost curve and a terrible guide to what the memory industry actually earned in the eighteen months that followed.

## Where the Claim Lands, and Why the Tape Shrugged

Buyers were back within hours. Nasdaq 100 futures are up 1.23% at 29,752.75 after the index lost about 5.5% last week, and S&P 500 futures are up 0.43%. Micron, SanDisk, Western Digital and Seagate were all higher pre-market, with SanDisk up close to 4%. Nvidia and AMD were firmer. Korea itself closed up about 1.75% at roughly 5,767 on the Kospi despite the chip names being down, which tells you the selling was concentrated rather than general.

The counter-headline is the one worth more than the headline. Nvidia has reportedly asked Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron to use TSMC's 3 nanometre process for the base dies of its next generation custom high bandwidth memory. That is not a company planning for less memory per rack. It is a company moving the logic die of its memory stack onto leading edge foundry capacity, which is an expensive decision that only makes sense if you expect the content per system to keep rising. Micron has already contracted most of its 2027 high bandwidth memory supply.

Put those side by side and you have the honest state of the argument this morning. One side is a published model architecture with a dramatic number attached and no third party verification. The other side is a procurement decision by the largest buyer in the industry, made with money, pointing the opposite way. We weight the second more heavily than the first, which is why this is a reopened debate rather than a reversed thesis. It is also why we think today's bounce is a reasonable response rather than a complacent one.

## The Number That Explains Last Week Better Than Any Headline

Bank of America put the hyperscaler cost of capital at roughly 10%, against about 4% in 2023. That single figure does more work than any of the narrative we have written in the last fortnight.

Capital spending is a return question, not an enthusiasm question. A project pencils when the return on new capital clears the cost of that capital, and the hurdle these companies are clearing has roughly doubled in three years while the revenue arriving against it is still mostly in the 2030s. That is the arithmetic behind record default insurance at CoreWeave and Oracle, behind a $5bn Volta loan clearing at 95 cents, and behind an eight session decline in semiconductors that ended last week as the longest losing streak in that index since 2008.

The cross-asset behaviour last week makes the point more sharply than any spread does. The ten year yield fell about 17 basis points while the Nasdaq 100 lost 5.5%. Falling yields are normally oxygen for long duration equities. They were not, because the move was a flight to safety rather than an easing of financial conditions, and because for this particular complex the relevant discount rate is not the Treasury curve but the credit spread stacked on top of it. Goldman's traders have artificial intelligence credit spreads, and hyperscaler spreads specifically, at the widest levels of the year.

Treasuries are a little better behaved this morning, with the ten year at 5.08%, the thirty year at 5.43% and the two year near 4.70%. October 28 hike odds sit under 15% and December is close to fully priced. Barclays moved to looking for quarter point hikes in both December and January, having previously forecast December alone, which is a reminder that the dovish repricing of the last three sessions was about the meeting date and not about the destination.

## China Grew Faster Than Expected and Still Has a Demand Problem

Third quarter gross domestic product rose 4.5% against a 4.4% consensus and 4.3% in the second quarter, with quarterly growth of 1.2%. September industrial production was 5.5% and retail sales 1.1% against a 0.5% forecast. Fixed asset investment is down 7.2% year to date, new home prices are 2.8% lower than a year ago and capacity utilisation sits at 73.1%.

The composition is the story and it is not a comfortable one. Growth came from factories and from exports, which are running at record levels and are themselves partly a function of the global artificial intelligence build. Investment is contracting at a pace that would read as a recession in most economies, and property prices are still falling. Retail sales beat a low bar rather than a high one.

What this does is remove the case for aggressive fourth quarter stimulus, and that matters more for commodities and for European exporters than it does for American equities. It also quietly undermines one of the more comfortable assumptions in global forecasts, which is that Chinese weakness provides a disinflationary offset. An economy exporting record volumes into a tight global goods market is not exporting deflation. The CSI 300 was flat at 4,174.53 on the print and the Shanghai Composite added 0.2%, which is the correct response to a number that beat for reasons nobody in Beijing would have chosen.

## France Is a Fiscal Story Wearing a Public Order Costume

Roughly 550,000 people protested across France over the weekend, with about 2,400 arrests, one death, town halls burned and hundreds of businesses looted. Prime Minister Lecornu has asked regional authorities for partial curfews and has not excluded a state of emergency. Unrest spread to Milan, Lisbon, Madrid, Bologna and Brussels.

Markets priced the fiscal consequence rather than the disorder. The French ten year rose about 10 to 12 basis points toward 4.90%, the spread over Germany widened to roughly 146 basis points and the CAC 40 fell about 1.35% with BNP Paribas, Société Générale and Crédit Agricole all down between 2.2% and 2.6%. The Stoxx 600 is near 640.7 and down about 0.63%, the DAX off 1.1% and the FTSE 100 down about 0.94%.

The logic is straightforward and it is worth stating because the headlines obscure it. A minority government facing a 2027 budget it must pass, with two of its recent predecessors already destroyed by budget talks, has just been handed a cost of living revolt and instructed by the President to open cost of living negotiations this week. Every plausible resolution of that costs money. Bond markets are not pricing riots, they are pricing the probability that the deficit path gets worse before a presidential election, and selling banks is the cleanest way to express it.

This is the second week running that European outperformance has rested on a compositional argument about not owning the artificial intelligence complex. That argument is intact. What today adds is that the alternative is not risk free either, and that the region's fiscal politics can produce its own repricing without any help from semiconductors.

## Energy: Washington Chose Cheaper Diesel Over Sanctions

Brent is near $108 and down about 1.7%, WTI $97.84 and down 1.19%, giving back most of Friday's near 4% gain. The move came from Washington rather than from the Gulf.

Trump signed an executive order waiving the operative Russia provisions of the Graham Act two days before they took effect, including the tariff authority of up to 500% on Russian goods, alongside a Treasury waiver permitting Russian diesel sales into April 2027. Russia is to supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel initially. Criticism was bipartisan, lawmakers have introduced a bill to block the diesel purchases, and Zelensky called the move wrong while saying he expects further relief.

Strip out the politics and the trade is legible. The administration has decided that the binding constraint on American inflation is the distillate market, and it has paid for relief in that market with sanctions leverage. That is consistent with everything the physical data has been saying for a fortnight, which is that crude is a 2027 surplus story and diesel is a right now shortage story. It also tells you how the White House ranks its problems five weeks from the midterms.

None of this fixes the shipping lane. Two tankers caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz after striking mines, more vessels were hit in the Gulf and off Yemen, the Houthis struck Yanbu over the weekend, and explosions were reported at Jeddah, Jazan and Abha. Iran's foreign minister says the seven day Hormuz reopening proposal still stands and Washington is waiting on a response to its counterproposal. Gulf crude flows are running near 80% of pre-conflict levels. Ukraine sent more than a thousand drones at Russia including a large strike on St Petersburg, and a primary unit at the Ryazan refinery is down. Supply risk on both ends of the barrel is unchanged. What changed is the price of the marginal diesel cargo.

## Earnings: A Bank Morning Again, With the Real Test on Wednesday

Nothing in the compute, memory, optical or launch complex reported overnight. Today is regional banks and industrials, and the week only becomes informative tomorrow evening.

**Fifth Third** earned $828m available to common shareholders, with reported diluted earnings of $0.90 and adjusted earnings of $1.13 against roughly $1.08 expected, on revenue of $3.28bn against about $3.34bn. Non-interest income fell about 5% sequentially while rising 29% year on year. Management says the full $850m of Comerica expense synergies is locked into a fourth quarter run rate and guided fourth quarter net interest income up 2.5% to 3.5%.

Our take is that this is an integration quarter that worked and a revenue quarter that did not, and the market is right to treat the shares as roughly unchanged. The beat is a cost beat, delivered by a merger the company controls, against a top line that fell short. For a regional bank in this curve that is a perfectly respectable outcome, and it is also the third consecutive week in which the differentiator across American bank results has been balance sheet and expense management rather than credit quality. Nobody is losing money on loans. They are losing it on securities books assembled when the ten year was nowhere near 5.08%.

The prints that matter are Netflix tomorrow after the close, then Tesla, Meta and IBM on Wednesday. Meta is the one to watch for this note's purposes, because it is the cleanest public statement available on whether a hyperscaler intends to spend into a 10% cost of capital. Traders bought puts on Friday at one of the highest rates of the year, so positioning into those prints is already defensive.

## Deals Reappeared, Which Is Information

Three announcements this morning, and the common feature is that all three are cash deals funded with debt in a week when debt was supposedly unavailable.

**Merck KGaA** agreed to buy **Silence Therapeutics** at $20 per American Depositary Share, about $1.3bn of equity value and $1.1bn of enterprise value, a premium of roughly 130% to Friday's close, financed with new debt and structured as a UK scheme of arrangement closing in the first quarter of 2027. The asset is divesiran, a phase three ready small interfering RNA therapy for polycythaemia vera that produced an 88% response rate against 19% on placebo in phase two. **Silgan Holdings** is up about 5.5% on an all cash agreement to acquire **Trivium Packaging** at roughly $3.4bn enterprise value. **EQT** agreed to acquire **Cleanaway Waste Management**, which surged on the news.

The read is narrow but genuine. Acquisition finance for pharmaceutical, packaging and waste assets is available on terms that let buyers pay a 130% premium. The funding problem this market has been pricing for two weeks is specific to assets whose cash flows arrive in the 2030s and whose collateral is a depreciating processor. It is not a general closure of credit. That distinction has been the backbone of this desk's framing since October 7 and it held again this morning.

## The Record

Friday's test is open and we are not going to pretend otherwise. We said Volta's new loan should trade at or below its 95 cent issue price through its first week in the secondary market, and that a print above 96 would mean we had overstated how expensive this funding has become. There are no reliable secondary quotes yet. The test carries to the end of the week on its original terms.

The larger correction is at the top of this note and we want it stated plainly rather than softened. On October 15 we wrote that the credit story retired the demand debate entirely. That was too strong. It was a claim about which question was more urgent, and we wrote it as a claim about which question was still alive. A weekend model release has made the demand question live again, and a desk that had declared it closed owns the cost of being surprised by it. The framing we should have used, and will use now, is that funding is the near term constraint and efficiency is the medium term one, and that the two interact.

We will also note what we got right into this morning, since the rule cuts both ways. We argued Thursday and Friday that this was a rotation inside the market rather than a withdrawal from it, and that the test was whether money leaving semiconductors reappeared elsewhere. It reappeared. The Dow gained on the week, energy and insurance absorbed the flow, and buyers were back in the Nasdaq complex within one session of the worst week since June.

One new test, to be marked on Wednesday evening. If the efficiency story has any purchase on actual spending plans, at least one of Meta, Tesla or IBM should soften its capital expenditure language rather than raise it. If all three guide capital spending flat to higher and say nothing about memory intensity, the DeepSeek release is a trading event and not a planning event, and we will say so.
