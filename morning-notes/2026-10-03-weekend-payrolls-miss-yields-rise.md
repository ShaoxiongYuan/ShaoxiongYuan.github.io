Saturday, October 3, 2026, written at 9:15 a.m. Eastern. This is a weekend wrap covering the week to Friday's close. US markets are shut and every figure below is settled rather than indicative. Mainland China and Hong Kong remain closed through October 7 and return on the 8th. OPEC+ meets tomorrow.

## Top Call: The Curve Rejected the Dovish Read Within Hours, and Nobody in Equities Noticed

September payrolls added 29,000 jobs against a consensus near 90,000, with 60,000 cut from July and August. October hike odds fell from roughly 70% on Monday to between 16% and 21% by Friday morning. Then the two year closed five basis points **higher** at 4.839%, the ten year five higher at 5.281% and the thirty year two higher at 5.629%.

Read that sequence twice. We published Friday morning with the two year at 4.716% and the ten year at 5.176%, taken four minutes after the release. By the bell both had not merely retraced, they had closed above Thursday. That is a twelve basis point round trip in the front end on the single most dovish labour print of the cycle.

The equity market kept the morning's conclusion and discarded the afternoon's. The Nasdaq Composite closed at a record 27,190.86, up 1.19%, Nvidia printed an all-time high, and the S&P added 0.73%. Gold fell 0.95% and ended the week down 3.6%. Bitcoin touched $87,000 in the morning and faded to roughly $84,845. Every asset that should rally on a lower real discount rate finished lower, and the index that depends on the longest duration cash flows finished at a record. Our view is that this is one crowded trade carrying an index, not a market pricing easier policy.

## Rates: What the Bond Market Actually Heard

The reconciliation is not complicated, and it is not a contradiction.

John Williams and Philip Jefferson both spoke this week and both leaned against October. Williams said there was no need for urgency and that the committee has time to gather information. He also said he sees one more hike **late this year**. The market priced the first half of that sentence and ignored the second. October going from 70% to 18% is not the hiking cycle ending, it is the hiking cycle moving to December, and a December hike is roughly as restrictive as an October one for anybody holding a ten year bond.

Underneath the meeting arithmetic sits the week's cleanest data point, which almost nobody traded. ISM manufacturing prices paid jumped 6.8 points to 77.9 on Thursday, with 58.6% of respondents reporting higher input prices and no commodity category reporting a decrease. Eurozone flash inflation printed 3.8% on Friday with energy at 18.8%. Average hourly earnings grew 3.0% against a headline CPI of 3.4%. A long end that rose on a payroll miss is pricing exactly that configuration: weaker employment, firmer prices, and a central bank that has told you it is slowing down rather than stopping.

The ten year touched 5.34% intraday during the week, its highest since 2002, before settling at 5.281%. The two to ten spread compressed back toward 44 basis points as the front end gave up its rally. That is not a growth scare being priced. It is a term premium that will not move for a labour report.

## The Cost of Capital Is the Story, and It Showed Up Twice This Week

Two items landed within forty eight hours of each other and they are the same item.

**Third quarter global M&A fell 41% from the second quarter to $993bn**, the first sub-trillion quarter since the second quarter of 2025, on LSEG data. Only ten deals above $10bn were announced, the fewest since the fourth quarter of 2024. Year to date volume is still up 28% at $3.9 trillion, the highest since 2001, but deal count fell 8% to levels last seen in 2020, so the annual figure is a handful of very large transactions rather than a broad market. US and European dealmaking fell sharply while Asia Pacific rose 8% sequentially to $242bn. BNP Paribas put the cause plainly, flagging that rising diesel prices and rates are prompting hesitation specifically on technology and AI related deals.

**Amazon is exploring moving about $8bn of Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips into a special purpose vehicle** and leasing them back, per the Financial Times. The SPV would raise debt from outside investors, who could take up to a 10% equity stake, while Amazon keeps operational control. This sits against 2026 capital expenditure of roughly $220bn and free cash flow that has turned negative.

Put those side by side. Deals that need financing are not getting done, and the largest buyer of accelerators in the world is engineering a structure to keep the accelerators off its balance sheet. Both are the same long rate expressing itself. Our read is that the leaseback is the more informative of the two, because it is a company with an AAA-adjacent balance sheet and $220bn of annual capex deciding that the reported asset base, not the cash, is the constraint worth managing. Credit analysts will treat those lease obligations as real claims regardless, so the economics do not change. What changes is what the reported return on capital looks like while the buildout runs.

This is the September 26 framing arriving in filings rather than in commentary. The market is sorting companies on whether they own capacity, rent it, or must fund it. Amazon has just moved itself one category to the right on purpose.

## Equities: A Record Index and a Negative Week

The week's scoreboard is the thesis in four lines. The Nasdaq rose 0.6%. The S&P fell 0.3%. The Dow fell 1.3%. The Russell bounced 0.94% on Friday but on a rate move that reversed before the close.

Friday's record was built by a narrow group. **Nvidia** reached $237.88 intraday for its first record since May, lifting market value to roughly $5.7 trillion and completing a 25% rebound from its late July low after a drawdown that had erased more than $1 trillion. The board added $150bn to the repurchase authorisation, taking the total to $235bn through fiscal 2028, which the company called the largest single increase in its history. AMD added 2.58%.

A $150bn buyback authorisation is worth pausing on. It is a capital allocation statement from the company with the best visibility in the complex, and it says the cash cannot be deployed into supply faster than it arrives. For a business whose constraint everyone agrees is capacity, that is either a signal that the capacity bottleneck is downstream of Nvidia, in power and packaging and optics, or a signal that management sees the shares as the better use of marginal dollars. Neither reading is bearish on demand. Both are a little awkward for anyone arguing Nvidia is still supply constrained in a way it can spend its way out of.

**Micron** fell 1.50% on Friday after Thursday's 3.03%. That is now two sessions in which a company that beat, guided a quarter 8% above the street and disclosed a $10bn jump in contracted customer commitments has added about 1.5% in total. We said Thursday that memory has moved from a contrarian position to a consensus one. Friday was the confirmation.

## Corporate: The Prints That Matter

**Tesla** delivered 486,532 vehicles in the third quarter against a consensus near 461,100, with production above 464,000 and energy storage deployments of 13.7 GWh. Deliveries rose 1.3% sequentially and fell 2.1% from a third quarter 2025 that was inflated by the rush ahead of the federal EV credit expiry. The shares added about 4%. The delivery number is a clean beat and the storage number is the one to watch, because 13.7 GWh came in light against expectations and storage is the segment most directly levered to the data centre power problem rather than to the car cycle.

**Nike** is the week's most important consumer datapoint and it is being treated as a single stock story. Adjusted earnings of $0.48 beat $0.44, revenue of $11.21bn missed $11.35bn, and Greater China fell 26% for a ninth consecutive quarterly decline. The full year guide of $1.15 to $1.35 against a $1.61 consensus is below every published estimate rather than below their average. Attached to it is a restructuring called Pace, running to 2031, targeting $2.5bn of savings including $1bn of employee related costs, with workforce decisions beginning in calendar 2027.

**Optics had the best week in the complex and it was policy driven.** Coherent rose 10% and Lumentum 9% on Thursday after reports that Washington is moving against Chinese optical transceivers, with a bipartisan Senate bill targeting Eoptolink and Zhongji Innolight and FCC restrictions expected to phase in starting at the 3.2T node. Deutsche Bank noted Lumentum's 200G lasers are sold out at roughly double the prior generation's selling price, with an optical circuit switch backlog above $400m. Both companies now put the AI optics market at $90bn by 2030 against $18bn in 2025. The light layer is where the physical bottleneck has migrated, and it has just been handed a protected domestic market on top of a shortage.

Elsewhere: **Paramount** closed $52bn of debt financing for the $110bn Warner Bros. Discovery acquisition, which is the one large transaction that did clear in a quarter when financing closed for everyone else. **Corteva** set out a separation into two companies worth about $50bn. **onsemi** restructured its **Synaptics** purchase to all cash at $123 a share for $5.7bn. **OpenAI** dismissed three safety staff for sharing confidential information and notified more than 100 organisations of unauthorised agent activity after examining roughly 50 petabytes of data. **Google** put its first TPU satellite into orbit under Project Suncatcher on SpaceX's Transporter-18. **Meta** faces a New Mexico request for $35bn to $40bn in civil penalties after a jury found more than 43 million violations. Prosecutors arrested a California company owner over more than $300m of export controlled GPU servers routed to China, and EU regulators are expected to designate Azure and AWS under the Digital Markets Act with a draft decision in November. In healthcare, Bristol Myers Squibb won an expanded Camzyos label including paediatric patients and Edwards Lifesciences won approval for the first size-adjustable surgical pulmonary valve for children.

**Anthropic** committed $100m to train 10,000 engineers through a residency style programme by the end of 2027, with its Nasdaq listing still targeted for October at a reported $2 trillion. If that prices anywhere near the target it will be the single largest repricing event of the quarter, and it will land in a market where the long end is at 5.28%.

## Energy: Brent Did the Work, WTI Round-Tripped

Brent settled at $99.68, down 2.57% on the day and about 4.4% on the week. WTI traded as low as $88.95 in the morning and settled at $91.11, down 1.90%. We flagged WTI as down roughly 7% on the week at 9:30 Friday. It closed the week down a fraction of that. The intraday low was not the week, and we should have said so with a settle rather than a screen print.

The structural point is unchanged and is now four weeks old. Crude is loosening on inventory builds, demand downgrades from OPEC and the IEA, and partial recovery in Gulf flows. Refined product is not. The diesel crack first cleared $100 a barrel on August 17 and set a record at $107.35 on September 1. Distillate inventories were 107.9m barrels in mid September, the lowest September level since 1982. The Gulf Coast jet crack hit a record for the series back to 1990. Thursday's ISM prices paid print cited petroleum based products among its drivers. Anyone reading $91 WTI as energy disinflation is reading the wrong barrel, and the manufacturing survey is now the evidence rather than the crack spread alone.

OPEC+ meets Sunday and is expected to leave November quotas unchanged, with Gulf output still far below targets and the group's attention on 2027 baselines. On the conflict, Iran struck four tankers in two days in late September, the fifth through ninth vessels since September 23, all transiting dark off Musandam. Tehran's blacklist is now 81 vessels. The pattern is pressure on the shuttle fleet rather than an attempt to close the strait, which is why Brent sits at $99 rather than $110 after a week with no diplomatic progress at all. China's suspension of October refined fuel exports outside Hong Kong and Macau remains the single most underpriced supply event in the product market.

## Asia and Europe

Tokyo gave back the Micron move, with the Nikkei falling 745 points, or 1.08%, to 68,211 after Thursday's 3.3% surge. The explanation is the simple one. Thursday repriced the whole semiconductor supply chain on a guide, Micron itself then closed up 3.03% rather than the 8% it was indicated, and Tokyo marked back toward where the cash market settled.

Seoul closed above 7,000 for the first time in five sessions at 7,003.74, up 0.46%, ahead of National Foundation Day. Foreign investors sold again and the domestic bid absorbed it again, with net foreign selling of 214.5bn won in SK Hynix and 202.7bn won in Samsung, though at a visibly smaller scale than earlier in the month. Our read has not changed. This is a currency and rate differential problem in the two names that produced a record $60.3bn September semiconductor export month, and it resolves when the American long end does, which Friday says is not yet.

Europe closed firmly, with the DAX up 1.21% to 25,241.14, the FTSE 100 up 0.3% to 10,459.95 and the Stoxx 600 higher near 632. That is a continent rallying on an American payroll number on the morning its own inflation printed 3.8% with energy at 18.8%. The divergence we have run for four sessions is now stark. American inflation was revised down by a change in how three service categories are measured. European inflation rose six tenths in a month because the bloc imports every barrel it burns. European equities are trading the global discount rate, not their own price level, which says the ECB is still not regarded as a live threat. The October 16 final CPI, with full detail attached, is where that gets tested.

Hong Kong and the mainland were shut for the entire data week and return Wednesday into an ISM services print, FOMC minutes and three sessions of accumulated news.

## Accountability

**We published Friday with the two year at 4.716% and treated it as the day's signal, and it closed at 4.839%.** The framing was right and the timestamp was wrong. We wrote that a market moving fifty points of hike probability on consecutive mornings was chasing headlines rather than processing information. Friday's afternoon was that same market reversing itself inside a session, which is the sharper version of the point and we did not get to publish it. The practical lesson is that on a release day the 9:30 print is a reaction, not a price, and a weekend wrap is where the settle belongs.

**The crude call was framed on an intraday low.** We flagged WTI down roughly 7% on the week. It settled down about 1.9% on the day and Brent took the weekly loss at 4.4%. The direction was right and the magnitude we published was the worst tick of the morning. We are tightening that to settles going forward.

**The distillate thesis is correct on mechanism for a fifth week.** Crude loosened, product did not, and the ISM manufacturing survey now cites petroleum based products directly. After three weeks of naming the wrong trigger we are content to let the mechanism stand without predicting what sets it off.

**The gold and bitcoin carry framework passes, in the uncomfortable direction.** We restored it to full confidence Friday morning on a move lower in nominal yields. By the close yields were higher, and gold fell 0.95% with bitcoin down 1.99% from an $87,000 high. The framework held both ways inside one session. It is working.

**The memory call is amended and stays amended.** Micron has now added about 1.5% across two sessions on a quarter that beat on every line and a guide 8% above the street. We said Thursday that this is consensus rather than a contrarian position. Friday's 1.5% decline is the second piece of evidence and we are no longer treating memory as an underowned trade.

**The breadth counterexample did not survive the week.** We noted Friday morning that the Russell was the strongest of the four contracts and owed the record a mention. It closed up 0.94% on a rate move that had fully reversed by the bell, and the Dow lost 1.3% on the week against a Nasdaq up 0.6%. The breadth problem is intact, and one morning of rate sensitivity was not a reversal.
