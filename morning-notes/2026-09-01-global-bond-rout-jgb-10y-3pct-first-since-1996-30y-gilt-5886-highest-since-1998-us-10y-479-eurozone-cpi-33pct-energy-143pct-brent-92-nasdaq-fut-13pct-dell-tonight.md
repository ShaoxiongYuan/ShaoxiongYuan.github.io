Tuesday, September 1, 2026, written at 9:20 a.m. Eastern, before the cash open and on the first session of the new month, so every US number below is a future or a pre-market print and can look different by ten o'clock. One thing happened overnight and it happened everywhere at once.

## Top Call: The Long End Broke in Four Countries Simultaneously

Japan's ten-year touched 3.00% for the first time since September 1996. Britain's thirty-year gilt jumped nine basis points to 5.8856%, its highest since March 1998. The German ten-year reached 3.35%, the highest since 2011, and the French ten-year 4.21%, the highest since 2008. The US ten-year pushed to roughly 4.79%, the highest since January 2025, with the thirty-year at 5.27%.

The two-year did almost nothing anywhere. That is the whole call. This is not the market repricing anybody's next policy meeting. It is the market repricing the price of long-dated government credit, and it did so in every major sovereign at the same time on the same three inputs: an energy shock that is now visibly in the inflation data, fiscal deficits nobody is consolidating, and a corporate bond calendar that has turned the AI buildout into a competing claim on the same duration.

Bloomberg's global government debt gauge yields 3.72%, the highest since 2008. Australia's ten-year had its sharpest single-day rise in five months. Prashant Newnaha at TD Securities called it "a genuine regime change" in global fixed income, and for once the phrase looks earned rather than promotional, because a synchronised move of this size across four separate policy regimes cannot be explained by any one of their central banks.

Kenneth Broux at Societe Generale put it more usefully: the Middle East conflict has "effectively killed" the idea that Kevin Warsh's hawkishness would stabilise yields. Tai Hui at J.P. Morgan supplied the other half, noting that "the stalemate in the Middle East risks pushing energy prices higher, meanwhile, few actions have been taken to consolidate fiscal deficits."

## Why This Is Not a Fed Story

Friday's move after Warsh was a bear flattening. The two-year rose twenty-three basis points on the week to 4.348% while the thirty-year fell six to 5.21%, and this note read that correctly at the time as a market paying up for a credible inflation target. Today is the mirror image. The two-year is roughly where Warsh left it and the thirty-year is six basis points above Friday's level at 5.27%, which means the entire move is term premium.

That distinction is not academic. A curve that bear flattens is telling you the central bank will do the work. A curve that bear steepens globally, on the same day, in countries with four different inflation rates and four different policy stances, is telling you that the marginal buyer of thirty-year government paper wants to be paid more to hold it regardless of who runs the central bank. Warsh cannot fix that and neither can Ueda.

The supply side of it deserves more attention than it gets. US debt has passed $40 trillion. Against that, hyperscaler dollar debt has more than doubled since last September to above $360 billion, 2026 issuance had already passed the full-year 2025 record by mid-year at roughly $159 billion, and Morgan Stanley expects global AI-related issuance near $570 billion this year. Every one of those bonds is long-dated, investment grade and competing for the same duration bucket as a thirty-year Treasury or a thirty-year gilt. The AI capital cycle has quietly become a rates story, and this is the first session where you can see it in the price of sovereign debt rather than in credit spreads.

Which is also why Nasdaq futures are down 1.31% against the S&P's 0.66% and the Dow's 0.60%. The most duration-sensitive equity index in the world is being marked against a thirty-year yield that is breaking out, and the companies inside it are the ones issuing the paper.

## Japan Is the Sharp End

The Bank of Japan meets on September 17 and 18 with its policy rate at 1%, a level last seen thirty-one years ago after hikes from 0.5% to 0.75% in December and to 1% in June. Market pricing for a further move to 1.25% is carried at 73% on one caliber and 80% to 90% on another, and this note is listing both rather than picking one. The ten-year yield has more than tripled in two years and roughly doubled since Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi took office last October on a fiscal expansion platform that a lot of investors read as reckless.

The yen sits near 160 per dollar, which is the level that has drawn intervention before. And Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has now put Washington's thumb on the scale, saying he expects Governor Ueda to "do the right thing" and telling Japanese officials, per NHK, that "Japan's next move should be a rate hike."

Our take is that this is the single most underpriced macro variable on the board. Japan has been the world's supplier of cheap duration for a generation, and a three percent ten-year with a 1.25% policy rate coming changes the arithmetic for every Japanese life insurer and bank that has been funding foreign bond purchases. The repatriation channel is the mechanism by which a Japanese policy meeting shows up in the US thirty-year, and it is now open. Watch the yen rather than the JGB for the signal, because 160 is where the two policy authorities disagree.

## Eurozone Inflation Is Energy and Only Energy

The August flash print came in at 3.3%, up from 2.9% in July and exactly in line with expectations.

| Component | August | July |
|---|---|---|
| **Headline** | 3.3% | 2.9% |
| **Energy** | 14.3% | 10.3% |
| **Services** | 3.0% | 3.3% |
| **Non-energy goods** | 1.2% | 0.9% |
| **Food, alcohol, tobacco** | 1.2% | 1.2% |

Read the second and third rows together. Energy inflation accelerated four full points and accounts for the entire headline increase. Services inflation, the component the ECB actually watches because it is the domestic wage pass-through, fell three tenths to 3.0%. Underneath an alarming headline the core process is still cooling.

That is the most important nuance in the data this morning and the bond market ignored it, which tells you what kind of selloff this is. A market pricing a genuine inflation reacceleration would have sold the front end. A market pricing an energy shock it expects central banks to look through, on top of deficits it does not expect anyone to fix, sells the back end. It sold the back end.

The energy line has a physical source and it is worth naming. Dutch TTF front-month gas rose 1.3% to EUR 71.30 per megawatt hour, the highest since March, with European gas up roughly 130% this year. Storage is about 62% full, some seventeen percentage points below the five-year seasonal average, going into the refill season. Roughly 20% of global LNG trade moves through the Strait of Hormuz and a large share of it originates in Qatar. Europe's inflation problem and Europe's security problem are now the same problem, and the ECB has no instrument for either.

## The Gulf: Two Tankers, Eight Minutes

Two vessels were struck on Monday night. The Saudi-flagged Sidr was hit roughly seventeen nautical miles northeast of Khasab at about 7:52 p.m. local time. The South Korean-owned Senegal Prosperity, operated by Sinokor Maritime, was struck by three unknown projectiles at 8 p.m. Eight minutes apart is not a coincidence.

CENTCOM separately rejected an IRGC claim to have mined a supertanker, stating flatly that "no ships have hit mines in the Strait." President Trump called the conflict "a relatively little war," described Iran as a "failed nation," said "we're going to hit them hard," and posted an AI-generated video depicting Kharg Island being blown to pieces. President Pezeshkian offered to reciprocate if Washington returns to the June ceasefire memorandum, and Iran's foreign ministry said it would "respond decisively."

Brent is near $92 and WTI is $87.98, up 2.59%, roughly thirty percent above pre-war levels. Daily transits through the strait remain below half of pre-war volumes.

The analytical point is that the escalation ladder here is now decoupled from the diplomatic one. Trump is threatening strikes while Pezeshkian is offering terms, which historically resolves toward the strikes. But the barrels matter more than the rhetoric, and what moved this morning was not a headline about intent, it was two hulls with holes in them. Insurance underwriters price hulls, not press conferences.

## Gold Has Now Failed Three Different Tests

Gold is $4,406.50, down 1.67%, with a second caliber at $4,432.20 as of 7:56 a.m. and December futures having opened up 0.4% at $4,498.70. Those three prints do not reconcile cleanly and this note is listing all of them rather than choosing.

The direction is not in dispute, and the direction is the story. Gold fell 3.13% on Friday when a Fed chair turned hawkish. It fell again on Monday when the United States and Iran exchanged fire for the first time in a month. It is falling again today on a global bond rout with the dollar softer. A metal that declines on a monetary shock, a geopolitical shock and a fiscal shock in three consecutive sessions is not a hedge against any of them.

This closes an argument these pages have been running since July. Through the summer gold bid on every headline touching the deficit, the long end or the Gulf, and the working hypothesis here was that this represented a debasement trade against an absent central bank. Warsh removed the absent central bank and the bid vanished immediately, including on days when a debasement story would have been the obvious reading. The bid was monetary. It is gone. Nobody should be carrying gold in a portfolio this week on the theory that it hedges fiscal risk, because Tuesday just tested that theory with a global fiscal repricing and gold went down.

## Asia and Europe

Asia sold the bond move and did it in tech. The Nikkei closed at 66,215.34, down 0.15% on one caliber and below 66,000 on another, with the damage concentrated in exactly the names most exposed to duration and AI capex: Tokyo Electron down 4.6%, Fujikura 3%, Advantest 2.9%, Taiyo Yuden 1.5%, Murata 1.4%. Hong Kong was the weakest major market at 25,329.73, down 0.93%. Shanghai held up at 3,979.89, down 0.16%, still above 3,950.

Korea again gave the most informative tape. The Kospi opened lower on US weakness and closed up 0.23% at 6,835.8, back above 6,800, with Samsung Electronics up 0.38% and SK Hynix up 1.14%. Retail, foreign and institutional investors were all net sellers. The index rose anyway, on corporate buybacks. That is the second consecutive session in which Korean chip names have absorbed a macro shock, and it is worth separating from sentiment: a buyback is a company with cash telling you what it thinks its own shares are worth, which is a different and better signal than a fund flow.

Europe is the anomaly worth flagging. The Stoxx 600 is near 661 and up about 0.1%, the DAX near 26,357 and the FTSE 100 near 10,864, all essentially unchanged, on a morning when the Bund printed a fifteen-year high in yield, the OAT a 2008 high and the thirty-year gilt a level not seen since Tony Blair's first term. European equities are declining to price their own bond market. One of those two assessments is wrong and the bond market is usually the one that is right.

## Earnings: Dell Is Tonight and It Is the Right Test

Dell reports fiscal second quarter results after the close against roughly $44.9 billion to $45.2 billion of revenue and about $4.89 to $4.91 in EPS, with the stock up roughly 140% this year and options implying a move near 10%.

| Dell FQ2 | Consensus | Last quarter |
|---|---|---|
| **Revenue** | about $45B | AI servers shipped $16.1B |
| **EPS** | about $4.90 | AI orders booked $24.4B |
| **AI backlog** | watch the print | $51.3B, up from $43B |
| **ISG margin** | the actual question | mid-single-digit target on AI systems |

**Our take**: the backlog number will beat and it does not matter. Dell has told the market it targets only mid-single-digit operating margins on AI systems, memory is scarce and getting more expensive, and conventional DRAM contract prices are forecast up 13% to 18% quarter on quarter in the second half. The only line that carries information tonight is the Infrastructure Solutions Group gross and operating margin, plus whatever management says about second half input costs. Nvidia guided January gross margin to 71% to 72% on memory. Marvell guided lower on custom silicon mix and fell 7% on a record quarter. Dell is the systems integrator at the end of that chain with the least pricing power of the three, which makes it the cleanest read available on whether AI suppliers keep the money they are winning. A beat on revenue with a miss on ISG margin is the bearish outcome and it is also the likely one.

Palo Alto Networks reports fiscal fourth quarter after the close as well, MongoDB and Medtronic report today, and NIO posts second quarter results. Broadcom on Wednesday remains the week's largest single datapoint, at roughly $29.4 billion with AI semiconductors guided to $16 billion and the fiscal 2027 "in excess of $100 billion" target the live question. Zscaler follows Thursday against guidance of $875 million to $878 million.

An ISM manufacturing print above 55 deserves more attention than it will get. American factories have now expanded for seven straight months and July was the strongest reading since May 2022. That is not the profile of an economy that needs help, and it removes the growth argument for patience at exactly the moment energy is pushing headline inflation up everywhere. If the ISM beats today, Friday's payrolls become the only thing standing between this committee and a hike, and the asymmetry stays where it has been all week: a soft number buys much less dovish relief than it used to at a 4.1% jobless rate.

China's Caixin manufacturing PMI prints tonight against July's 50.9 and June's 51.7, which is the private-sector cross-check on Monday's official 49.8.

## Accountability

Saturday's edition and Monday's could not reconcile two Hormuz transit calibers, AP's 24 vessels in a week against a pre-war norm of about 130 per day, versus 114 transits and a 73 per day baseline. Today's reporting supplies a third and more usable figure: daily transits are running below 50% of pre-war levels. That sits between the two and is consistent with neither exactly, but it is directionally clear and this note will use it going forward. The claim made in these pages in late August that the strait was normalising was too generous, and today's two tanker strikes settle the matter in the other direction.
