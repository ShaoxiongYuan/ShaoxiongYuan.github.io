## Top Call: The Buildout's Lenders Now Matter More Than Its Customers

Three artificial intelligence financings are in the market at once, and together they total roughly $150bn. Broadcom's banks launched syndication on Monday of a $60bn package behind Anthropic's chip leases, structured as a $42bn Class A senior secured tranche and an $18bn Class B junior tranche led by Blackstone, which is committing about $9bn of its own balance sheet. On Tuesday SpaceX was reported in talks for about $40bn, roughly $10bn of bank loans and $30bn of investment grade paper with Apollo expected to lead. On Wednesday the Journal reported Broadcom arranging more than $50bn for the custom accelerator it is co-developing with OpenAI under the Nexus programme, with Apollo and Blackstone again among the lenders approached and the first phase possibly nearer $30bn.

Yesterday we wrote that the important change was structural rather than directional: once the marginal dollar of accelerator purchasing is borrowed rather than earned, the buildout becomes rate sensitive for the first time. One session later the number has tripled. This is no longer a single preliminary conversation at one private company. It is a syndication calendar.

Two features of these structures deserve more weight than the headline sizes. First, the vendor is guaranteeing the customer's borrowing. Broadcom is partially guaranteeing the senior layer of the Anthropic deal, which lowers Anthropic's cost of capital and moves a slice of lease risk onto Broadcom's own A minus rated balance sheet, and Anthropic's prospectus flags the conflict of interest in Broadcom's dual role as supplier and lender. Second, the same two private credit names, Apollo and Blackstone, appear in all three deals, and Apollo is separately one of six institutions that partnered with Nvidia in August on compute financing platforms. Concentration of this kind does not make a cycle unsound, but it does mean that one withdrawal of appetite reprices three transactions rather than one, and it means a backlog is now partly a read on credit rather than purely on demand.

The test is today at 1:00 p.m., when Treasury reopens $22bn of thirty year bonds with the yield at 5.72%. Wednesday's $39bn ten year auction was excellent, stopping 1.7 basis points through when issued at 5.300% with a 2.77 bid to cover and indirect bidders at 80.3% against a 72.4% average, and dealers left holding only 2.54% of the paper against an 8.8% average. That is real demand, not a reluctant syndicate. If the long bond clears as cleanly, the argument that this curve has found a price gets stronger and the $150bn stack is financeable. If it tails, every spread product behind it widens, and the AI complex is now a spread product.

## Rates: Unanimous Hawks, One More Hike, and a 7.49% Mortgage

The September minutes landed at 2:00 p.m. yesterday and were more hawkish than the stale instrument we expected. All participants backed the twenty five basis point move to 3.75% to 4.00%, and of the eighteen officials who submitted projections, sixteen expected another increase by year end. Chair Kevin Warsh has not submitted a forecast since taking the chair in May, so the dot plot understates rather than overstates the committee's hawkishness. Governor Waller said this morning that further hikes will likely be needed, that inflation remains too high, and that the committee has flexibility on pace with no need to move at consecutive meetings. October odds sit near 17% and December is close to fully priced. That combination, a committee that intends to hike again but not next, is exactly what the curve has been saying for three weeks.

This morning the ten year is 5.34% and the thirty year 5.72%, up about six basis points each, with the two year near 4.81%. Wednesday's intraday highs were 5.36% on the ten year and 5.73% on the thirty, both twenty four year records, and the ten year closed at 5.28% only because the auction was strong. Two year to ten year is near 52 basis points and two year to thirty near 91, so the steepening continues to come from the back end.

Two prices tell you what this curve costs in the real economy. The thirty year fixed mortgage rose nineteen basis points to 7.49%, the highest since November 2023. New York Fed one year inflation expectations rose to 3.9% from 3.6%. High yield spreads widened seven basis points to 305. None of those is a crisis level. All three move in the same direction, and the third is the one that matters for the top call, because the AI stack is being underwritten into a credit market that is getting slightly more expensive every week rather than cheaper.

Initial claims came in at 197,000 against a 200,000 consensus with the prior week revised to 199,000, which is a labour market that is not generating layoffs even as the payroll prints have gone soft. Bank of America card spending slowed to 3% year on year in the week to October 3 from 5.6%, and consumer borrowing moderated on a fall in revolving credit. Read together with PepsiCo's guidance cut below, the household picture is a consumer who still has a job and has stopped adding to the tab.

## Energy and Geopolitics: A Strike Window Before November 3

Brent is $105.02 and up 4.8%, WTI $92.13 and up 4.4%. Brent settled at $100.20 on Wednesday, so this is a five dollar move overnight and the first print above $104 since the escalation began.

The cause is a calendar rather than a barrel. Axios reports that the Pentagon directed Central Command several days ago to complete preparations for a possible return to major combat operations against Iran, and the Atlantic reports that the White House asked for strike options executable before Election Day on November 3. No decision has been made and the president has not approved anything. What has changed is that the market now has a dated window rather than an open ended standoff, and the reported target set is energy infrastructure, critical infrastructure and nuclear sites. Trump told a rally in San Antonio on October 6 that he is no longer interested in a deal, Qatari mediators are still waiting for an Iranian answer to an American counterproposal, and Iran is reported to be assuming an attack and debating whether to strike first.

The physical tape matches. A tanker was hit off Qatar, the first strike deep inside the Persian Gulf in about a month, which matters because it is past the chokepoint rather than at it. The Houthis claimed a ballistic missile attack on King Khalid airport in Riyadh. Unconfirmed reports describe explosions in Riyadh and smoke at Abqaiq. CENTCOM rejected Revolutionary Guard claims of full control of Hormuz and said commercial traffic including 20m barrels of crude is moving, while Iran and Oman agreed coordinates for safe transit routes and Saudi Arabia is in talks to formalise shuttle services through the Strait. Separately, Hurricane Isaias is strengthening in the Gulf of Mexico with producers shutting in roughly 500,000 barrels a day and evacuating staff.

The policy response arrived one day too early to matter. On Wednesday the IEA agreed to accelerate the remaining releases under its March emergency plan, about 100m barrels on top of the 325m already delivered, and to prioritise diesel where possible, with roughly 1.1bn barrels of emergency stocks still held including more than 200m barrels of diesel. Brent fell on the headline and settled at $100.20. It is $105 this morning. JPMorgan was right that the 100m barrels is not 100m barrels of new intervention, because the volume was already committed in March, and the EU and Germany both confirmed they would release only what was approved then. A reannouncement of existing commitments is worth about one session against a strike window.

The French detail is the one we would put in front of a macro committee. Prime Minister Lecornu said on Wednesday that France will release 10m barrels from its strategic diesel reserves to ease cost of living pressure and calm student protests that have left dozens injured and hundreds arrested. That is a strategic petroleum reserve being used as a domestic political instrument, not a supply instrument. We have argued for seven weeks that the shortage lives in refined product rather than at the wellhead, and the evidence has now moved from a crack spread to a producer's price list to a government agency's forecast and now to a European government trading diesel barrels for street calm. Gasoil is above $191 and European TTF gas is above €80 per megawatt hour.

## Asia: Korea Printed the Record and Sold Harder

Samsung reported preliminary third quarter operating profit of 107.4 trillion won, up about 783% from 12.17 trillion a year ago and the first quarter above 100 trillion won in South Korean corporate history. Revenue was about 195 trillion won, up roughly 127%. The shares fell 2.4% and the Kospi fell 2.62% to 6,625.93, closing below its fifty day moving average.

We flagged this setup yesterday and it resolved harder than expected. The operating number beat the LSEG SmartEstimate of 106.1 trillion by about 1.2% and missed the higher sell side marks near 108.7 trillion, while revenue came in under forecasts clustered between 199 and 207 trillion. So the market is paying for the revenue line, not the profit line, which in a memory upcycle is a statement that investors want volume growth confirmed rather than price per bit celebrated. Our reading yesterday was that a market selling its two largest companies into its best ever print was expressing a view on the won and on global duration rather than on semiconductors. We will now qualify that: with revenue short by several trillion won, part of yesterday's foreign selling looks like information rather than macro. We were too quick to assign all of it to duration.

TSMC is the counterweight and it is unambiguous. September revenue was NT$511.86bn, down 0.6% from August but up 54.6% year on year, against August's 53.3%. Third quarter revenue of about NT$1.49tn beat the NT$1.46tn consensus and was up roughly 39%. Taiwan's September exports rose 60.9%. Yesterday we set the test as whether TSMC decelerated against August while the assemblers posted records, which would have meant the complex was converting inventory rather than adding capacity. It accelerated. The constraint is where Lisa Su and Sanjay Mehrotra keep saying it is, which leaves the funding question as the only open one and puts the whole weight of the bear case on the top call rather than on demand. TSMC shares still fell 1.4%, before the revenue release.

Tokyo fell 1.42% to 69,042.11, back below 70,000. Hong Kong fell 1.43% to 23,785.79, its lowest since July 7. The CSI 300 returned from the holiday and fell 1.09% to 4,310.28, with the Star 50 at a six month low intraday, so the mainland reopened and immediately marked itself to the week it missed. Singapore fell 3%, India 1.6%, Australia 0.8% on miners, and MSCI Asia Pacific was down as much as 1.8%. The Tokyo Stock Exchange announced it will cut Topix constituents by about 40% to 986 names, the largest reshuffle it has run, which is a flow event worth diarising for Japanese small and mid caps.

## Europe: Banks, France, and a Euro at a 17-Month Low

The Stoxx 600 is 625.65 and down 0.80%, after falling 1.00% to 630.26 on Wednesday. The DAX is down about 0.80% from 25,104.36, the FTSE 100 down 0.4% from 10,458.50, and the CAC 40 is in correction. Energy and utilities are leading, banks, health care and construction lagging, with 409 Stoxx members down and 180 up.

Wednesday's session in Europe was a bank event. The Euro Stoxx Banks index fell 3.38% to 295.93 with Societe Generale and Deutsche Bank both down more than 5%. The proximate cause is French fiscal risk, and the OAT to Bund spread widened to a 142 basis point session high this morning, the widest since the 2012 eurozone crisis, with the ten year OAT near 4.83% against a 3.52% Bund. Lecornu is trying to cut about €43bn of public spending through a parliament where he has no majority, the budget debate starts October 13, and Bank of France Governor Moulin said the conditions for ECB intervention are not met, which is the correct answer and also the one that leaves the spread where it is. The euro is 1.1182 and at its lowest since May 2025. Meloni faces a confidence vote in Italy and Rome is finalising a 2027 budget while asking banks and energy groups for state contributions.

The dollar index is 102.38 and the Bloomberg dollar gauge is near a three month high. We wrote on Monday that the dollar's strength was being priced in Paris rather than Washington, and it has now been three sessions in which the euro has made lows on French fiscal news while the American long end has also made highs. Both legs are dollar positive and they are independent, which is why this move has been more durable than a rate differential story would suggest.

## Earnings: The Ones That Matter

**Applied Digital** is the cleanest read of the week on the top call. Revenue was $341.9m, up 322%, against a consensus near $111m, with adjusted revenue excluding ChronoScale of $300.4m. The GAAP loss attributable to common holders was $221.0m or $0.76 a share against an expected loss near $0.27, while adjusted net loss was $4.1m or a penny, and adjusted EBITDA was $64.4m. Data centre rental revenue was $79.1m including $65.8m of base rent; services revenue was $262.8m, of which about $157.2m was tenant fit-out and $23.0m was GPU hardware sales. The company also announced a 210 megawatt fifteen year lease with an investment grade hyperscaler worth roughly $5.2bn of base term contracted revenue.

| Metric | Consensus | Actual | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | About $111m | $341.9m (+322%) | Fit-out, not rent, drove the beat |
| GAAP EPS | Loss near $0.27 | Loss of $0.76 | $221m loss; adjusted loss a penny |
| Adjusted EBITDA | Not guided | $64.4m | Rent base only $65.8m |
| New contract | None assumed | 210MW, 15 years, about $5.2bn | Investment grade counterparty |

**Our take**: the gap between a $341.9m revenue line and a $65.8m base rent line is the whole story. Most of this quarter's revenue is construction work billed to tenants, which is lumpy, low margin and not the annuity the equity is valued on. The $221m GAAP loss against a $4.1m adjusted loss is where the cost of capital shows up, and that is the number to watch next quarter now that the sector's marginal funding is priced off a 5.72% long bond. The 210 megawatt investment grade lease is genuinely good news and is the right kind of asset to borrow against. We would rather own the contract than the construction revenue.

**Samsung** and **TSMC** are covered in the Asia section. **PepsiCo** reported revenue up 5.6% to $25.27bn, slightly ahead, with adjusted earnings of $2.34 against $2.29, and cut full year core constant currency earnings growth to 2.5% to 3% from the low end of a 5% to 7% range. Tariff refunds contributed $178m. That is now the third consecutive consumer print this week, after Lamb Weston and Constellation Brands, where the beat is a cost or tariff item and the forward number goes down. **Levi Strauss** fell about 3.4% on the slowest direct to consumer growth since late 2022. **Crocs** is worth a glance for a reason unrelated to its quarter: Croslite foam is more than 80% of its materials and is largely oil derived, so a $105 Brent is a cost of goods event for it.

## Accountability

**The financing thesis is working and we should say what would break it.** We wrote yesterday that the buildout had become rate sensitive. Twenty four hours later the number went from $40bn to roughly $150bn and the vendor guarantee structure emerged, both of which strengthen the argument. The honest statement of the risk is that private credit appetite has been deeper than almost anyone expected all year, and Wednesday's ten year auction was a reminder that duration demand at these yields is real. If today's thirty year stops through and spreads hold, our thesis is early rather than right, and we will say so.

**We assigned too much of Korea's selling to macro and not enough to the numbers.** Yesterday we said a market selling Samsung and SK Hynix into the best print in Korean history was expressing a view on the won and duration rather than on semiconductors. The print came with revenue several trillion won short of consensus and the Kospi fell another 2.62%. The duration leg still stands, but part of what we called macro was investors who had a better revenue estimate than the sell side average. That is a real correction, not a hedge.

**The TSMC test resolves in favour of the capacity story and we did not predict the direction.** We asked whether September revenue would decelerate against August's 53.3%. It accelerated to 54.6%, which settles the inventory question against the bears. We set the test rather than called it, and we claim no more than that.

**The IEA call was right for one session and that is becoming a pattern we should name.** We have now watched a Saudi price cut, a G7 release and an IEA acceleration each buy one session before the next incident took the premium back. The rule we should write down is that supply side policy responses to this conflict are worth roughly one session, because none of them changes the freight, insurance or refining constraint. The crude discipline rule has paid a fifth round trip in five sessions.

**The distillate thesis keeps getting confirmed in places we did not forecast.** We expected the refining constraint to show up in cracks and inventories. It has now shown up in a French prime minister releasing 10m barrels of strategic diesel to calm student protests. We will take the confirmation and note that we had no view on the political channel at all.

**The breadth claim stands after two sessions in its broad form.** We restored it yesterday after the Russell fell on the S&P's record day. Wednesday the Russell fell 1.31% against the S&P's 0.22% and this morning the small cap contract is again the weakest of the four. Two sessions is not a verdict, but the narrowing we withdrew has not asked to come back.
