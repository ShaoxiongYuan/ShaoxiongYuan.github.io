Thursday, July 23, 2026, and the verdict the whole tape was waiting for arrived after last night's close, but it was not the clean green light the bulls wanted. Alphabet and Tesla, the first mega-caps of the season, both fell hard even though the businesses held up, because each one raised the price of admission to the AI race and reported negative free cash flow in the quarter. US stock futures are lower, with S&P 500 and Dow futures each off about 0.4 percent and Nasdaq 100 futures down near 0.5 percent as the Big Tech pair drags. Underneath that, though, the demand side of the trade is roaring: Asia went the other way overnight, with South Korea's Kospi up 3.5 percent behind SK Hynix and Samsung, and ServiceNow jumped about 7 percent after hours on an AI-fueled beat. The second story is oil, which has opened a genuinely new front. Iran-backed Houthis struck two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea on a twelfth night of US strikes, giving the market a two-chokepoint problem and sending Brent up roughly 4.6 percent toward $97. Intel reports tonight and the ECB decides at midday.

## Top Call: The Capex Bill Comes Due, but the Demand Is Real

The one thing to carry into the open is that last night answered the question we flagged all week, and the answer was messy in a way that is actually clarifying. Alphabet beat on revenue, grew cloud 82 percent to $24.8 billion, and then lifted its 2026 capital spending guide toward $205 billion from a prior top near $195 billion, and it printed negative free cash flow. Tesla grew revenue 26 percent but missed badly on earnings and also burned cash. The market read both as the same thing: the buildout is not slowing, it is accelerating, and for now that shows up as cost rather than payoff. That is why both stocks are down, Alphabet about 3.6 percent and Tesla about 5.5 percent premarket, and why futures are soft. But do not confuse the price reaction with the fundamental signal. The reason the capex is going up is that the demand is there, and the clearest proof came from the other side of the trade overnight. The Kospi ripped 3.5 percent because SK Hynix and Samsung sell the memory that all that spending buys, and ServiceNow beat and raised its AI revenue target because enterprises are paying for the software layer on top. Our read is that this is a healthy repricing, not a thesis break. The names that get paid first in a capex surge are the picks-and-shovels suppliers, and they are confirming demand even as the hyperscalers absorb the bill. We stay constructive on memory, compute and AI infrastructure and would use the mega-cap wobble to lean into the supplier layer, while respecting that a market this focused on free cash flow can punish the spenders for another quarter or two. Written pre-market, so levels will move by the open.

## Big Tech: Spending Up, Free Cash Flow Down, and the Market Sends a Bill

The headline number of the night was Alphabet's capex line, and it moved the wrong way for sentiment even as it validated the demand story. Guidance to as much as $205 billion for 2026, up from a prior ceiling near $195 billion, tells you Google sees enough AI and cloud demand to keep pouring concrete, and the 82 percent cloud growth backs that up. The problem for the stock is that the spending, stacked on the quarter's outlays, tipped free cash flow negative, and a market that spent the first half of the year rewarding AI optionality has abruptly started asking when the return arrives. Tesla told a similar story from a different starting point. Revenue rose 26 percent on strong volume, but adjusted earnings came in well below the Street and the company also reported negative free cash flow, leaving the stock down more than 5 percent before the bell.

Our take is that this is the pivot the whole cycle had to face eventually, and it is better that it comes now with demand clearly intact than later when it might not be. The bear case is simple and not crazy: if the biggest and best-capitalized buyers of AI compute are burning cash to build, the discount-rate math on the entire chain gets harder, especially with the ten-year back near 4.66 percent. The bull case is that negative free cash flow driven by voluntary, demand-led investment is a choice, not a wound, and that Alphabet raising its capex again is the single loudest confirmation that the compute gets built regardless of the open-model noise that started this month's rout. We would separate the two clearly. The spending is bullish for the suppliers and a show-me story for the spenders, and the tape today is trading the show-me half.

## The Demand Side: Asia Chips Rip and ServiceNow Proves the Software Pull

While US futures sag on the capex reaction, Asia delivered the counterpoint in real time. South Korea's Kospi surged 3.5 percent, with SK Hynix up about 5.3 percent and Samsung up about 4.3 percent, as the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index's overnight strength fed straight into the memory names that supply the buildout. Tokyo followed, with the Nikkei up about 0.6 percent near 66,500 as Advantest and SoftBank led, though profit-taking in Fast Retailing and Kyocera capped the gains. The message from the region that actually manufactures the AI supply chain is that order books are full, which is exactly what Alphabet's capex guide implies at the other end of the pipe.

The software layer said the same thing. ServiceNow beat with earnings of about 90 cents against an 85-cent estimate and revenue near $3.99 billion, and it has been guiding its Now Assist AI revenue target higher through the year, sending the stock up around 7 percent after hours. Our take is that ServiceNow is the cleanest tell in the group precisely because it is not a chipmaker: it shows enterprises paying real money for applied AI, which is the demand that eventually justifies the hyperscaler capex the equity market is fretting about this morning. The attention now turns to Intel after tonight's close, where the setup is different and lower-stakes for the scheme, a foundry turnaround and CPU story into a Street looking for about 19 cents on roughly $14.4 billion of revenue. TSMC's recent note that agentic AI is reviving the role of CPUs in data centers is the one thread that could make Intel more than a sideshow.

## Oil and Geopolitics: A Second Chokepoint Opens

Crude is the other force pulling on this tape, and overnight it got materially more dangerous. Iran-backed Houthi militants struck two Saudi oil tankers, the Encelia and the Layla, in the Red Sea, setting fires on both, the first vessel attacks since the group announced a blockade of Saudi-linked shipping through the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. That came alongside a twelfth consecutive night of US strikes on Iran, and President Trump warned he would hold Tehran responsible for Houthi action and promised major military punishment if shipping is targeted again. Brent jumped about 4.6 percent toward $97, its highest since late spring, and WTI rose near $90, with at least seven vessels already rerouting to avoid the strait.

Our take is that the geometry of the oil risk just changed, and that matters more than the price print itself. For weeks the market could tell itself this was a single-chokepoint story at Hormuz, a shipping-risk premium that would unwind the moment a ceasefire looked real. A second front in the Red Sea turns it into a two-chokepoint problem, and two independent points of failure are far harder to price as a fear trade that simply fades. As long as barrels are still flowing, this remains a premium rather than a supply loss, but the probability that it becomes a genuine loss has risen, and a Brent price near $97 feeds directly into the headline inflation math that keeps a July hike on the table. We read the oil move as the most important macro input on the screen today, ahead of any single earnings line, and every Red Sea or Hormuz headline is now a two-way gap risk.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran, Hormuz and now the Red Sea:** The dominant macro risk widened materially overnight. A twelfth night of US strikes plus Houthi attacks on two Saudi tankers in the Red Sea have created a second chokepoint alongside Hormuz, pushing Brent toward $97. Trump's threat to hold Iran responsible for Houthi action and to deliver major punishment raises the odds of further escalation, not less.

**Global shipping:** At least seven vessels have diverted to avoid the Bab el-Mandeb Strait after the Houthi blockade of Saudi-linked traffic. Rerouting lengthens voyages and lifts freight and insurance costs, a slow-burn goods-inflation channel that compounds the crude spike.

**Trade policy:** The 25 percent Brazil tariff that went live yesterday, the earlier 50 percent Canada threat, and a floated generic-drug levy remain a compounding second inflation front. It is quieter than oil this morning but still building into the FOMC.
