Monday, October 26, 2026, written at 9:15 a.m. Eastern, fifteen minutes before the open. Every equity figure below is an indicative pre-market mark and will move. Futures are higher across the board, the Nasdaq contract leads, crude is giving back about 2% of last week's 6% gain, and the most violent price action anywhere on the screen is in Brazilian assets. The Federal Open Market Committee decides Wednesday, Microsoft and Alphabet report the same afternoon, and September core PCE lands Thursday, the day after the decision.

## Top Call: The AI Build Is Switching From Debt to Equity, and the Sorting Rule Is Visible

Meta filed this morning to raise up to $95bn of equity. The structure matters more than the headline. About $35bn comes underwritten now, split into $22.5bn of Class A common sold through forward sale agreements and $12.5bn of three-year mandatory convertible preferred. The remaining $60bn is an at-the-market programme in non-voting Class C shares that cannot begin until those shares start trading, expected around the first quarter of 2027. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have indicated interest in buying up to $5bn of the Class A. The shares are up 2.4% pre-market after falling 7% Thursday and 9.4% last week, and Morgan Stanley kept its overweight and lifted its target to $720.

Set that next to the two other funding events of the weekend. Elon Musk said SpaceX has closed $40bn of debt for graphics processors, explicitly crediting its investment-grade standing and its revenue from Starlink and launch, with Bloomberg reporting pricing talks were still open. In the same posts he said debt markets have almost entirely shut off access to capital for any company without substantial revenue independent of artificial intelligence data centres, and that the more than two hundred neoclouds face what he called guppy-level death and consolidation. He added that SpaceX has no interest in buying one.

Read those three together and the picture is not a funding crisis. It is a sorting mechanism, and the sorting rule is non-AI cash flow. Meta has $82.45bn of cash and securities against $80.92bn of long-term debt and an already-stretched balance sheet, so it is going to the equity market instead and accepting the dilution. SpaceX has launch and Starlink revenue, so it can still borrow at investment grade against GPUs that will not arrive until the first half of next year. CoreWeave, Nebius and IREN have neither, and they are being priced accordingly: five-year protection on CoreWeave sits somewhere between roughly 1,088 and 1,115 basis points against the 1,000 level that S&P treats as distress, its 2032 unsecured paper trades below 80 cents for a yield above 15%, and its 2033 bonds and Nebius's 2034s both printed record lows. CoreWeave is down about 36% this month, IREN about 32%, Nebius about 27%.

Our view is that this is the most important structural development in the capital expenditure story since the summer, and it is a repricing of the cost of capital rather than a verdict on demand. The uncomfortable detail is the anchor list on the Meta deal. Three semiconductor suppliers indicating interest in up to $5bn of their largest customer's equity is the same circular arrangement the market has spent two months discounting in Nvidia's dealings with OpenAI and CoreWeave, just at a larger scale and now sitting above the debt in the capital structure rather than inside a purchase guarantee. Nvidia already carries $36bn of off balance sheet cloud commitments, $105bn of guarantees on OpenAI data centres, and an obligation to buy up to $6.3bn of CoreWeave capacity if CoreWeave cannot find another taker. Equity funding is genuinely safer than leverage for Meta's own solvency. It does not make the vendor financing loop any less circular, and the people pricing Meta's own five-year protection appear to agree, because it sits near 120 basis points, roughly double where it traded in August.

The other number worth holding onto is capital spending guidance of $135bn to $145bn for this year against a third quarter that burned $2.12bn of cash, Meta's first negative free cash flow in more than a decade. A company does not launch the largest equity programme in its history because financing is comfortable. It does it because the spending plan is fixed and the debt market has become the expensive option.

## Brazil: Positioning Was Wrong and the Clearing Price Is Brutal

Lula won Sunday's runoff with 51.79% of valid votes against 48.21% for Flávio Bolsonaro. Brazilian equities had hit a record high after the first round on the assumption of a change of government, so this is a positioning event as much as a policy one, and it is clearing in the thinnest hours available.

The marks so far: the Brazil country fund is down about 27% pre-market, XP down roughly 48%, StoneCo down 35%, PicPay down 34%, PagSeguro down 32%, Nubank down between 23% and 25%, and MercadoLibre down 14%. The dollar gained roughly 8.7% against the real at the open, Ibovespa futures fell close to 15%, and B3 widened its daily trading limits on rates, currency and the index for the session. ING looks for double-digit losses on the cash index and a real somewhere between 5.20 and 5.25, while Goldman has flagged the possibility of a 10% currency move. Barclays thinks sovereign spreads could widen around 300 basis points in the coming days. The read-through to Europe is narrow but real, with Santander down 4% and Telefónica down 7% on the result plus a JPMorgan downgrade.

Two things we would separate. The first is that financial names are taking twice the pain of the index, which is a credit and regulation trade rather than a growth one, and it tells you what the market thinks a fourth Lula term does to spreads and to payments regulation. The second is that Flávio Bolsonaro is contesting the result and calling for annulment. That keeps the volatility bid alive past the first session and means the finance ministry appointment, not the vote tally, is the next thing that actually prices. We would treat today's offshore prints as an overshoot in instruments that were never designed to clear a surprise election, and we would not confuse the size of the gap with the size of the policy change until that appointment is made.

## Rates: A Hold Is Not the Question, the Dissents Are

Wednesday's decision at 2:00 p.m. Eastern is close to settled. Futures put a hold at 3.75% to 4.00% near 87%, with roughly 13% on a hike, and December remains priced anywhere between 75% and 90% depending on the day. Chair Kevin Warsh will not pre-commit, so the information content sits in two places: whether Hammack, Kashkari and Logan repeat the hike dissents that produced July's nine to three vote, and whether the statement acquires language that reads as tightening without being guidance.

We wrote on Saturday that the committee has been handed an energy-led price impulse and no fresh read on the trend, because September core PCE and third quarter output do not arrive until Thursday. That has not changed, but this morning's tape softens the impulse slightly. Crude is down about 2%, the ten-year is unchanged at 4.99%, the two-year is a basis point higher at 4.69%, and the curve flattened about two basis points at both the two to ten and five to thirty points. A flattening into a meeting where the near-term outcome is fixed is the market pricing a committee that stays restrictive for longer rather than one that is about to move. Core PCE is tracked near plus 0.36% month on month, which is not a number that lets anyone relax.

The supply calendar is heavy enough to matter on its own. There is $95bn of thirteen-week and $82bn of twenty-six-week bills at 11:30 a.m., a $69bn two-year auction at 1:00 p.m., $70bn of fives tomorrow and $44bn of sevens Thursday, all of it into a week that also carries the European Central Bank, the Bank of Canada, the Reserve Bank of New Zealand and the Bank of Japan. Canada and New Zealand are both priced around 85% to 88% for a hike, which is worth noting because it says the hawkish turn is not an American peculiarity.

## Energy: Two Percent Comes Off on a Day Nothing Happened

WTI is down 1.91% to $101.30 and Brent down 1.80% to $114.62 after Friday's settlement of $116.72, the highest since April. The catalyst is the absence of one. President Trump said there would be no strike on Iran yesterday, and spent Sunday framing the choice as a deal or, in his words, finishing it off, while saying a strike could come as soon as today. Qatari mediators were in Tehran on Sunday, and Reuters reports that senior Saudi, Turkish and Pakistani officials were due to meet Iranian counterparts there today, which we would treat as single-sourced until confirmed. Iran's parliament continues to say no talks are possible until its seven conditions are met, and the seven-day roadmap to reopen the Strait of Hormuz that Washington rejected in late September is still formally on the table.

This is the clearest evidence yet for the argument we have been making for two weeks, which is that the risk premium in crude is attached to transit and to optionality rather than to interrupted production. A barrel market that gives back 2% because no strike happened on a particular Sunday is a market paying for the possibility of closure, not for a closure that occurred. Nothing physical improved overnight. The strait is still shut, a tanker was struck near it on October 23, the Houthis claim attacks on pumping stations along the Saudi East-West pipeline, and the International Energy Agency's Fatih Birol has said he sees no credible path back to pre-war energy levels in 2027. European gas is going the other way, with Dutch front-month up about 3% near EUR 97 per megawatt hour and pressing EUR 100.

The practical conclusion is that the $117 print was never a floor and the $101 print is not relief. With effective spare capacity near a quarter of a million barrels a day, the price will keep trading the probability of a strike, and the probability has a date on it, because the midterms are eight days away and the reporting continues to point at a campaign resuming afterwards.

**Verizon** is a better print than the revenue line suggests. Adjusted earnings of $1.31 grew 8.3%, adjusted EBITDA rose 8.0% to a record $13.8bn, postpaid phone net additions came in at 406,000, and the company raised full-year adjusted earnings guidance to $5.04 to $5.06 for a third consecutive quarter while taking subscriber guidance to 1.1 million to 1.2 million and free cash flow growth toward 12%. Revenue of $34.31bn missed a Street that was somewhere between $34.8bn and $35.8bn. The part relevant to this note is the agreement worth more than $2bn with Amazon Web Services to expand network connectivity and distributed computing. A carrier monetising fibre and edge capacity to a hyperscaler is the other side of the capital spending trade, and it is the cleanest way we have seen a telecom name attach itself to the compute build without underwriting any of it.

**EQT and Range Resources** is the deal of the morning and the market is treating it as dilution. Range holders receive 0.987 EQT shares, implying $54.91 and a 20.6% premium to Friday, for about $12.83bn of equity inside a combined enterprise value above $50bn, with EQT holders retaining roughly 73% and closing expected in the first quarter of 2027. EQT fell 4.7% and Range rose 10.1%, which is the usual all-stock pattern, but the strategic logic is worth stating plainly. The single largest incremental source of American electricity demand is data centre load, the single largest marginal fuel for it is Appalachian gas, and this creates the biggest producer of that fuel at a moment when the equity market is punishing anything that spends to serve compute. Buying the molecule rather than the rack is a different expression of the same demand, and it is being financed with paper rather than debt, which is this morning's theme twice over.

**Tonight is the real test.** Cadence Design Systems and Amkor Technology both report after the close, and both are more informative about the artificial intelligence cycle than anything that printed this morning. Cadence sells the design software every accelerator and custom chip passes through, so its backlog is a forward read on silicon that has not been taped out yet. Amkor does advanced packaging, which is the physical bottleneck that sits immediately behind high bandwidth memory in the supply chain. Nucor also reports, and steel for data centre construction is the least glamorous and most literal read on whether the buildings are still going up. Last week's lesson from Western Digital and Lam Research was that clearing consensus is no longer sufficient when the marginal buyer is asking who finances the building. We would expect both of tonight's prints to be strong and neither to be rewarded for it.

## Asia and Europe: Technology Weak in Seoul, Politics in Beijing

Asia was mixed and the weakness was concentrated in the places with the most artificial intelligence beta. The Kospi fell between 0.9% and 1.3% as technology dragged, the Nikkei closed down 0.80% at 62,189.65, the CSI 300 slipped 0.52% to 4,084.03 and the Shanghai Composite was close to flat, while the Hang Seng added 0.48% to 23,203.94 and the ASX 200 gained 0.2%. Indonesia fell 1.5% and Taiwan was closed. The yen is the weakest major currency ahead of Friday's Bank of Japan decision, with dollar-yen up 0.3% near 157.30.

Beijing opened its Fifth Plenum today, and the framing in much of the coverage is wrong in a way worth correcting. This is not the five-year plan meeting. That was last October's Fourth Plenum, and the National People's Congress approved the fifteenth plan in March. This plenum is about party governance and is expected to formalise one of the largest senior personnel turnovers in modern Chinese political history. For markets that means the agenda items investors care about, which are rebalancing toward consumption and the campaign against what Beijing calls involutionary competition, are already written down and this week is about who executes them.

Europe is firmer. The Stoxx 600 is up 0.40% at 638.10, the DAX up 0.94% near 25,419 and the CAC up 0.9%, with Spain the exception at minus 0.5%. The German Ifo came in at 90.2 against 89.9 expected, which is a small beat but the right direction. The French spread story continues to vindicate the mechanical part of Saturday's argument even though the rating call went against us: the ten-year gap over Germany has narrowed to about 158 basis points from Friday's 176 basis point peak after Moody's affirmed Aa3. Genmab is up as much as 13%, Bayer 3.3% and Roche 1.9%, while GTT is down as much as 15% and BASF 1.8% lower on a Deutsche Bank downgrade. Sterling is lagging into Wednesday's Autumn Budget.

## The Record

One call from Saturday resolved against us, two tests remain open, and today adds a third.

**France stays resolved against us.** We framed Friday's note around an expected Moody's downgrade and the agency affirmed Aa3 instead. We said so on Saturday and we are repeating it here because the market consequence showed up today: the spread over Germany narrowed about eighteen basis points, which is precisely the forced-selling catalyst we said was coming and that never arrived. The underlying argument that the bond market had priced the fiscal position weeks before the agency did is intact. The directional call was still wrong.

**Test one stays open, due Friday.** We said that if CoreWeave's five-year default swap is still above 1,000 basis points at the close on October 30, the funding stress is structural rather than an artefact of a thin dealer market, and that a move back below 800 without new financing would mean we overweighted one illiquid instrument. The spread is near 1,100 this morning and the company's bonds are at record lows, so the test is live and pointing toward confirmation with four sessions to run.

**Test two stays open, due Wednesday and Friday.** Our claim has been that the equity drawdown in the artificial intelligence build is itself a tightening of financial conditions that substitutes for policy. The test as written: a hold on October 28 with December hike odds ending the week below 60% and the ten-year still under 5.10% confirms the substitution read, while December above 80% with the ten-year above 5.15% kills it and makes this a sector event rather than a macro one. The ten-year at 4.99% satisfies one leg this morning. December pricing in the high seventies does not satisfy the other, so this still resolves on Warsh's language.

**Test three stays open, due Wednesday and Thursday.** We said we would treat the memory trade as a duopoly margin story rather than a scarcity rent if Samsung's and SK Hynix's disclosures confirm Samsung has taken the high bandwidth memory revenue lead. SK Hynix reports Wednesday with consensus near KRW 76.96tn of operating profit on KRW 98.23tn of revenue, an implied margin above 78%. Bernstein has HBM revenue up 11% sequentially to $7.6bn and Samsung ahead, while Korean reporting suggests HBM4 shipments may have fallen short for a second consecutive quarter on the timing of Nvidia's Rubin rollout. Those two claims are not compatible and Wednesday settles it.

**New test, this week.** If equity, convertible and at-the-market issuance by hyperscalers and their suppliers continues to displace debt, we expect at least one more large capital expenditure programme to be funded primarily with stock before the end of November, and we expect five-year protection on the issuers that do it to tighten rather than widen afterwards. If Meta's own spread widens from roughly 120 basis points despite a $95bn equity raise, then the market is pricing the spending plan rather than the balance sheet, and the distinction we drew in the top call collapses.
