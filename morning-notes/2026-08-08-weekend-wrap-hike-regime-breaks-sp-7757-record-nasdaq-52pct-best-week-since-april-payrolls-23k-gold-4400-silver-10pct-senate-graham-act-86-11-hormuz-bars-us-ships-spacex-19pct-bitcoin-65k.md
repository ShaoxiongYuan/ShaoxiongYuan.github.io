Saturday, August 8, 2026. Written after Friday's close, so every level below is a settlement rather than a moving mark, and the next thing that can change any of it is Monday's Asian open. The week ended with the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite at record closes, the Stoxx 600 at a record, gold up 7 percent, silver up 10 percent, and the reason for all of it being a payroll report that showed the American economy shedding jobs. That combination is the entire story, and it is worth being precise about what it means rather than celebrating the prints.

## Top Call: the Market Bought the Discount Rate, Not the Economy

July payrolls fell 23,000 against a consensus near 80,000, with 103,000 removed from May and June, and the market responded by taking the S&P 500 to 7,757.64 and the Nasdaq to 26,690.62, both records, capping the strongest week since April. Gold settled at $4,399.70 and silver at $63.50, up 7 and 10 percent on the week. Equities and precious metals rallying together on the same input is the signature of a pure policy repricing: the market is paying for a lower path of rates, not for better earnings. The one thing that would invalidate it lands Wednesday at 8:30, and it is the July CPI.

Breadth was real rather than mechanical. Roughly 336 S&P 500 names advanced against 167 decliners, and advancers beat decliners about two to one on both the NYSE and the Nasdaq. The VIX closed at 14.90, down 1.65 percent, which tells you the market treated a negative payroll print as a relief rather than a warning.

## Two Things We Got Wrong Yesterday

Credibility is worth more than a good call, so both corrections go at the top rather than in a footnote.

First, we wrote Friday morning that September hike odds had collapsed to roughly 20 percent from about 55 percent. That was the tape at 9:15am and it was too aggressive. The settled figures are around 44 percent on the CME FedWatch caliber and about 40 percent on fed funds futures. The direction was right and the magnitude was not. A move from 55 to 44 is a meaningful shift in the burden of proof, but it leaves September genuinely contested, and it means Wednesday's CPI matters far more than we implied yesterday.

Second, we wrote that Korea had reclaimed 6,400. It did in the first minutes of the session and then gave all of it back, closing at 6,258.77, down 0.60 percent, a second consecutive losing day. The bargain hunt we described was real and it failed to hold. That is a more informative outcome than the rally we reported, because it says the sold-out 2027 memory headline was not enough to repair Korean positioning after Thursday's 4.58 percent collapse. The buyer showed up and did not stay.

## What the Payroll Report Actually Says

The composition matters more than the headline. Local government education shed roughly 50,000 positions, leisure and hospitality lost about 40,000, and retail trade lost about 19,000. The unemployment rate improved to 4.1 percent from 4.2 percent while the participation rate fell to 61.4 percent, the weakest in more than five years. A rate that falls because the denominator shrinks is not an improvement, it is a measurement artefact sitting on top of a hiring freeze.

The revisions do more damage than the headline. Removing 103,000 from May and June re-levels the entire second quarter and puts the three month average of job creation close to zero. This is the difference between a noisy month and a changed trend, and it means the Fed spent the summer building a case for tightening against a labour market that was materially weaker than the data in front of it showed at the time.

Average hourly earnings at 3.2 percent year over year finishes the argument. The hawkish case was never really about crude, it was about crude passing through into wages and becoming embedded. Wage growth undershooting while participation falls is the opposite of that mechanism. What remains is an inflation impulse imported entirely through a blocked shipping lane, and the funds rate has no transmission channel into a strait.

## Gold and Silver Did the Honest Repricing

Gold added $100.10 on Friday to settle at $4,399.70, a 2.33 percent day and a 7 percent week. Silver rose 3.07 percent to $63.50 and gained 10 percent over five sessions. Silver outrunning gold by three percentage points on the week is the detail worth keeping, because silver carries an industrial demand component that gold does not, so a silver-led move is not purely a monetary hedge. It is a bet on lower real rates plus continued physical demand from the same electrification and semiconductor buildout that drove the equity tape.

The cleaner way to read the pair is this. Nominal yields fell modestly, with the two year down four basis points and the ten year down barely more than one. Precious metals moved far more than that. The gap is the market marking down expected real rates over a longer horizon, which is another way of saying it now expects the Fed to sit still or ease while energy keeps pushing the price level. That is a policy constraint being priced, and it is a more sober message than the one the equity indices sent.

## Sector Spotlights

**Semiconductors and memory.** The week's arc was violent and instructive. Monday delivered a 6 percent gain in the PHLX Semiconductor Index, with Marvell up 14 percent on its Flash Memory Summit product launch, Intel up 10 percent, Micron up 8 percent, AMD up 7 percent and SK Hynix up 6 percent. Wednesday night SanDisk and Western Digital printed the best storage quarters on record and were sold hard because guidance implied the price curve might flatten. Friday brought the industry report that Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron have committed all 2027 DRAM and HBM output, filling customers at only 60 to 70 percent of requested volume. Sold-out capacity two years forward with rationed allocation is a steepening price curve, not a flattening one. The lesson from a week this noisy is that memory equity is currently trading on guidance language rather than on physical supply, and those two things have diverged.

**Software.** Atlassian up 32, Cloudflare up 16, Twilio and JFrog firm, the Trade Desk down 29, and one day earlier Datadog down 20 and HubSpot down 24. Anyone still trading software as a single exposure had an expensive week. The organising question is whether a business sells the seats, capacity or distribution that agents consume, or sells an intermediation layer agents can route around.

**Space and orbital compute.** SpaceX rose 12 percent Friday and about 19 percent on the week, which is the cleanest positioning signal in the market right now. The first lock-up tranche released up to 911.5 million shares into a stock that had closed at an all-time low days earlier, and it went up anyway. That says insiders were not the marginal seller the market feared. The Starmind programme with Nvidia, targeting orbital compute payloads built on the Vera Rubin architecture with a first launch as early as 2027, gave the sector its narrative, and Rocket Lab and AST SpaceMobile both added roughly 5 percent alongside. Tranche two of 319 million shares comes Wednesday.

**Power and utilities.** Vistra committed up to $1.0 billion of its own equity to the Helix digital infrastructure vehicle alongside Nvidia, KKR and KIA, on a quarter that was otherwise a miss, with adjusted EBITDA of $1.767 billion against consensus near $1.79 billion. A merchant generator taking an ownership position next to the chip vendor converts the AI power trade from a contracting story into a capital structure story. Combined with lower rate expectations lifting utilities and REITs on Friday, power was the quiet winner of the week.

**Healthcare.** Two approvals worth logging. The FDA granted accelerated approval on August 6 to Replimune's Tudriqev, an oncolytic immunotherapy, and approved Takeda's Orzeyful for narcolepsy type 1 on August 7. The agency also named seven companies to its PreCheck pilot for accelerating domestic drug manufacturing capacity, which is a policy signal about onshoring rather than a single-asset event.

**M&A.** Deal flow reappeared in European telecoms rather than technology. The UAE's e& agreed to sell its entire Vodafone stake for $5.95 billion, and Iliad, Orange France and Bouygues assembled a joint bid worth roughly 20.35 billion euros for SFR. Consolidation bids of that scale getting assembled says financing conditions in Europe are considerably more permissive than the rate backdrop would suggest.

## Asia and Europe

Asia finished the week split along the memory fault line. Japan's Nikkei was effectively flat at 65,607, down 0.12 percent, with cheaper crude offsetting a second day of technology weakness. Hong Kong's Hang Seng added 0.44 percent and the mainland was the region's strongest performer, with the CSI 300 up 0.93 percent to 4,694.44. Korea was the exception and the tell: the Kospi opened strong, faded through the session and closed at 6,258.77, down 0.60 percent for a second consecutive loss after Thursday's 4.58 percent rout. Korea is now the market where the AI trade gets stress tested first, because index concentration in Samsung and SK Hynix means it carries the memory cycle without diversification.

Europe simply kept going. The Stoxx 600 closed at a record 660.25, up 0.3 percent, for a fourth consecutive weekly advance, with technology up 1.9 percent on the day and the best performing sector on the week. The DAX added 179 points, or 0.69 percent, to 26,319, and London and Paris both finished higher. The pattern we have flagged repeatedly still holds. European outperformance is not defensive rotation, it is levered participation in the same semiconductor cycle without Korea's memory concentration, which is why Europe compounds records in the weeks Korea takes the damage.

## Geopolitics: Congress Escalated, the Strait Did Not Open

Friday produced the most consequential legislative action of the year on this file. The Senate passed the Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 by 86 to 11, authorising tariffs of up to 100 percent on major buyers of Russian oil and gas and imposing sanctions on Russian officials. Named for the late senator who spent years pushing it, the bill now goes to a House that is on recess until September, where its path is genuinely uncertain given right-wing opposition to Ukraine involvement and Democratic objections to the tariff grant.

Two features make this more than symbolism. The first is that it covers Iran as well as Russia, which links the two live energy conflicts into a single instrument at the exact moment the Hormuz negotiation is unresolved. The second is that a House recess until September means the earliest plausible action coincides with the September FOMC and the next round of tariff decisions. Markets have a month to price it, which usually means they price it late.

On Hormuz, the terms deteriorated again. Iranian state media said the arrangement with Oman prohibits American and Israeli vessels outright, fines violators up to 20 percent of cargo value, denies passage to parties deemed to have harmed Iran until war damages are compensated, gives Iran management of navigation, traffic monitoring and maritime security, and remains conditional on Washington ending its blockade of Iranian ports. Oman has still not commented publicly on any of it.

This is the third consecutive week in which the deal has advanced procedurally and worsened substantively. Coordinates agreed, joint statement drafted, and simultaneously a term sheet Washington has already rejected in public. Crude fell roughly 7 percent on the week and then rose 1.3 percent on Friday into precisely this news, which is the market beginning to notice the gap. Selling crude on Hormuz headlines has worked for two weeks. It has been working on announcements of progress rather than on the movement of barrels, and only eight vessels transited on the most recently reported day against roughly 130 before the crisis.
