Saturday, June 27, 2026. There is no live tape this weekend. US equity and bond markets are shut until Monday, June 29, so the last cash prints on the screen are Friday's June 26 closes. Treat this as a stocktaking note rather than a reaction piece. The week that just ended was the moment the AI trade stopped being a one-way bet. The Nasdaq Composite fell for a fifth straight session Friday to close at 25,297.62, capping a roughly 4.6 percent weekly slide that was its worst stretch in months, while the S&P 500 ended the week down close to 2 percent at 7,354.02 and the Dow held up far better at 51,876.11. What makes this selloff different from the wobbles earlier in the spring is the reason behind it. The market is no longer arguing about whether AI demand is real. Micron just answered that with a record quarter. It is arguing about who pays for the build-out, how much of it is funded with debt, and whether public and private markets still have the appetite to keep writing the checks.

## Top Call: The AI Reckoning Is a Financing Story Now

The single thing PMs need to carry into Monday is that the AI debate has shifted from demand to financing, and that is a harder problem to wave away. For two years the bear case was that the spending would not be matched by real usage. This week's price action quietly conceded that point and moved to a tougher one. The hyperscalers are now committed to more than 452 billion dollars of combined 2026 capital spending across Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta, OpenAI has pledged something near 1.4 trillion dollars of compute over eight years against roughly 13 billion dollars of revenue, and a growing share of the data-center build is being financed with private credit rather than cash flow. Friday's trigger was a report that OpenAI may push its IPO into 2027, which the market read as the financing window starting to close. Our take is that this is a healthy and overdue repricing inside an intact secular trend, not the start of a fundamental unwind, but the distinction matters less than usual because a market this concentrated can fall a long way on multiple compression and credit anxiety alone. We stay constructive on the names with real earnings and real free cash flow behind them, and far more cautious on the parts of the complex that need continuous outside funding to keep the lights on.

## Semiconductors: A Round Trip From Micron's Blowout to a Funding Scare

The whiplash is the whole story of the week. Micron printed a genuine blowout on Wednesday night, with revenue smashing to roughly 41.5 billion dollars and gross margins held near 81 percent, and for one session the entire memory chain ripped. By Friday the market had moved on to the harder question and sold the group anyway. That is the tell worth sitting with. A record quarter from the cleanest read-through on AI memory demand could not hold the tape, because the thing being questioned is no longer the demand. It is the cost of the capacity and the durability of the funding that pays for it. Chip names led the declines all week, and the read is that investors have stopped paying any price for AI exposure and now want to see the cash flows and the balance sheets.

The cleaner expression of that shift was the OpenAI headline. A delay to 2027 would be a direct signal that the willingness to fund AI at trophy private valuations has cooled, and it lands right after SpaceX's own debut soured. SpaceX priced its 75 billion dollar offering at 135 on June 11, spiked past 225 intraday on June 17, and has since round-tripped to roughly 153, barely above its listing price. That round trip has become the cautionary tale the entire AI-IPO pipeline is now measured against. The demand for compute is not in question. The appetite of markets to keep underwriting the spending at any valuation clearly is, and that is the variable that drives the chip complex into the second half.

## The Debt Underneath the Boom Is the Real Worry

The most underappreciated thread of the week, and the one we think deserves the most attention, is how much of the AI build-out is now leaning on borrowed money. Morgan Stanley estimates global data-center spending will run near 3 trillion dollars between 2025 and 2028, with roughly half of it covered by private credit rather than corporate cash flow. A meaningful slice of the data-center debt being issued is rated BBB or lower, which means the financing of the AI cycle is increasingly a credit-quality question, not just an equity-valuation one. That is a different and more serious kind of risk than stretched multiples. Stretched multiples compress and the market moves on. A funding cycle built on lower-quality private credit can seize up if sentiment turns, and it tends to turn fastest exactly when growth expectations get marked down.

This is the connective tissue between Micron's record margins and Friday's selloff. The same scarcity that powered 81 percent gross margins is forcing device makers to raise prices, and the same enormous capital plans that justify the memory upcycle are the plans now being financed at the riskier end of the credit spectrum. Our read is that the AI trade has entered a phase where the balance sheet matters as much as the income statement. Names that can self-fund their spending out of operating cash flow should hold up better than names that depend on a steady drip of fresh capital, and the spread between those two cohorts is likely to widen if the financing mood stays cautious into the summer.

## The Fed: Kashkari Becomes the First Hawk to Say It Out Loud

The macro backdrop got more hawkish on Friday, not less. Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari, a core voter, became the first official to openly pencil in a rate increase this year, telling reporters that in March he had one cut penciled in for 2026 and that he has now flipped that to one hike. His reasoning was notable for what it did not lean on. He pointed past the Middle East to what he called broad inflationary pressure across the economy, with services inflation his particular concern, which suggests the higher-for-longer camp no longer needs the war to justify itself. Core PCE printed 3.4 percent in May, the hottest since October 2023, and that reading is now the anchor the hawks were missing.

The cross-asset response was telling and a little counterintuitive. Rather than spiking, the two-year yield actually slipped to around 4.13 percent and the ten-year held near 4.40 percent, because the sharp drop in energy prices pulled the near-term inflation path lower even as Kashkari talked tough. The dollar eased and gold rebounded back above 4,000 to roughly 4,025. The market is holding two ideas at once. It believes the Fed under Kevin Warsh is biased toward tightening, which is bad for the longest-duration, highest-multiple corner of equities, and it believes cheaper oil is doing some of the Fed's disinflation work for it. September hike odds eased to about 60 percent from 70 percent a week ago, which is the bond market's way of saying the energy offset is real even if the rhetoric is not softening.

## Oil: The Slide Toward $69 Extends and It Helps

Crude kept falling hard into the weekend, with WTI dropping about 3.7 percent to settle near 69.23 and Brent off roughly 4.3 percent to around 71.99, both pressing fresh multi-month lows. The drivers are unchanged and they are all pointing the same way. The Strait of Hormuz is open and busy, the US license authorizing Iranian crude sales is clearing a path for more barrels, and OPEC keeps adding supply into a trimmed demand outlook. Oil has now given back essentially the entire premium it built during the spring war. For portfolios this is the cleanest counterweight to everything else that went wrong this week. Cheaper energy is a direct disinflationary force and a genuine tailwind for the consumer, and it is the single most important reason the bond market can wave off Kashkari while equities fret about AI financing. The flip side is that the same falling crude keeps grinding down the energy equities that have been the market's weakest group all month, so the household benefit and the sector drag continue to run side by side.

## Geopolitics: The Iran Inspection Standoff Stays Stuck

The nuclear file remains the live tail under an otherwise calmer Middle East, and it did not move toward resolution this week. IAEA Director General Rafael Grossi continues to insist that the signed accord places Iran's nuclear material and facilities under agency supervision and that inspections will follow, while softening the timing by saying whether it happens in two days or ten days is important but not essential. Tehran continues to push back, with an Iranian deputy foreign minister stating that any access will be decided only within a final agreement and only after the other side moves to lift sanctions. The two governments that signed the framework are publicly contesting its central term inside a 60-day window meant to settle exactly these details.

Our read is that the market impact stays contained for now because the pieces that move oil, the open strait and the crude license, are still working, which is why energy keeps bleeding rather than spiking. But enrichment and the uranium stockpile are unresolved, and a breakdown on the inspection question is the one headline that could put a bid back under crude and rebuild the haven demand in gold that has been draining all month. It is the cheapest tail to keep on the radar precisely because nobody is pricing it.

## Asia and Europe: Last Week's Closes

The Friday tape abroad was a mirror of the US selloff, concentrated exactly where AI exposure is heaviest. Japan was the standout to the downside, with the Nikkei 225 dropping about 4.2 percent to 69,360.88 as SoftBank tumbled on the OpenAI delay report given its outsized stake in the AI complex. Korea was hit nearly as hard, with the Kospi sliding roughly 6 percent as Samsung and SK Hynix gave back the memory-led gains of just 48 hours earlier. Greater China held up better in relative terms, with the Hang Seng off about 1.8 percent to 22,671.86 and the CSI 300 down roughly 2 percent. Europe closed lower in sympathy, the Stoxx 600 down about 0.8 percent and the DAX off 1.3 percent to 24,671.22, with the chip names leading and ASML, Infineon and STMicroelectronics among the weakest. With New York now shut until Monday, this is the picture that sets up the reopen, and the pattern through the week was consistent: Asia led the moves, Europe followed at a fraction of the magnitude, and the damage clustered in the most expensive technology.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran:** The ceasefire framework holds and the Strait of Hormuz stays open and busy, but the inspection question is stuck in a public standoff. The IAEA says Iran agreed to readmit inspectors; Tehran says any access follows a final deal and sanctions relief. Enrichment and the stockpile remain the unresolved core and the live tail for oil, sitting inside a 60-day negotiating window.

**Ukraine-Russia:** Still structurally unresolved and overshadowed by the Iran track. Combat continues along the eastern axes, and a pending US security-assistance package is the item to watch for fresh headline risk.

**Taiwan Strait and South China Sea:** PRC coast guard pressure near the Pratas Islands continues at a low simmer with no major escalation. The pattern of incremental activity persists but stays contained.

**Our Take**: This is the most important earnings event of the quarter and the most instructive. Micron more than quadrupled revenue year over year and held margins near a record 81 percent, which is about as clean a confirmation of AI memory demand as the market could ask for. And the stock still rolled over with the rest of the chips by Friday. That gap between a perfect print and a falling share price is the whole message of the week. The bull thesis on demand was validated and the market sold it anyway, because the worry has migrated to the financing and the valuation, not the fundamentals. For the memory names with genuine pricing power, this is a buyable derating over time. For the broader complex, the cash flows now have to do the talking.

**Our Take**: Put these four lines together and the picture is a build-out whose ambition has outrun its self-funding capacity, with the gap increasingly filled by private credit at the riskier end of the ratings scale. None of it says AI demand is fading. It says the market has finally started pricing the bill. A 2027 IPO delay from the most-watched private name is the clearest sign yet that the financing side has turned skeptical, and that is the variable to track into the second half far more than any single earnings beat.
