Saturday, October 17, 2026, written at 9:10 a.m. Eastern. This is a weekend wrap covering the week to Friday's close. US markets are shut, futures do not reopen until Sunday evening, and every equity, yield, currency and commodity figure above is settled rather than indicative. The one exception is a Hormuz report that landed this morning and remains unverified. China's third quarter gross domestic product arrives Sunday night Eastern. The Federal Reserve is in its pre-meeting quiet period until October 28, so there is no official voice between here and the decision.

## Top Call: The Binding Constraint Moved From Credit to Leverage

At roughly 2:45 p.m. on Friday the volatility index was near 16 and the market was having an ordinary bad day. By the close it was 19.99, up about 21%, after touching 22.31 intraday for the first time in four months. The S&P 500 finished down 1.09% at 7,659.44 and the Nasdaq Composite down 2.14% at 26,140.03.

The trigger was not a data release, a Fed comment or a bond auction. It was a rumor that Value Aligned Research Advisors, the AI-focused fund that lost 44% in a single month in July, was being forced to liquidate. There is still no confirmation of that. The firm's filings show substantial assets, a person close to it said the fund had not shut down, and no wire service has stood the story up. The market paid for it anyway.

That is the single most useful piece of information from the week, and it is not the one we have been writing about. For nine sessions this desk has argued that credit is the binding constraint on the artificial intelligence build, and we still think that is right about the cost of the build. It is the wrong frame for the speed of a drawdown. Credit decides what next year's capacity costs to finance. Leverage decides how fast this year's prices fall when somebody has to sell. A market that will give up 1% of index value on an unverified claim about one fund's positions is telling you that the marginal owner of the crowded end of this complex is borrowing to hold it, and that the exit is narrower than the entrance was.

So we are adding a second constraint rather than replacing the first. The credit channel is slow, visible and priceable, and we can follow it through spreads and new issue terms. The leverage channel is fast, invisible until it fires, and the only honest way to monitor it is through realised volatility, dispersion and the correlation of crowded names on down days. All three deteriorated this week.

## The Dispersion Is the Week, Not the Drawdown

Put the weekly numbers next to each other. The Nasdaq Composite fell about 4.5%, its worst week since June, ending a four week winning streak. The S&P 500 fell about 2%, its fourth losing week in six. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 0.67%, its second consecutive weekly gain. On Friday itself the Dow lost only 0.34% while the Nasdaq lost 2.14%, and the equal weighted S&P 500 fell 0.4% against a 1.1% drop in the capitalisation weighted version.

That gap is the story. More than half of the S&P 500's Friday decline came from a handful of mega cap AI names. Software fell 4.8% as a group, with Snowflake down 8.4%, Palo Alto Networks 7.6% and Palantir 7.2%. Against that, ConocoPhillips rose 3.4%, Exxon 2.5%, Allstate 3.5% and Progressive 2.2%. Staples and defence gained. Apple closed up 0.4% at $343.46 and was the only member of the Magnificent Seven higher on the day.

We have called this a rotation rather than a liquidation since Thursday of last week, and the weekly tape supports that again. Money is leaving the part of the market that needs a syndicate to fund next year's capital plan and arriving in the part that generates cash from assets it already owns. An index that can absorb a 4.5% week in its most crowded sector while its industrial average makes a weekly gain is doing orderly work.

The qualifier matters more this week than last. Rotation and deleveraging look identical for the first two weeks and then diverge sharply. The distinguishing test is whether the money that leaves semiconductors shows up somewhere else or simply leaves. So far it is showing up, in energy, insurance and staples. If the next leg sees the Dow and the equal weighted index fall with the Nasdaq rather than against it, that is the signature of leverage rather than preference, and the read changes.

## Memory, Storage and the Neoclouds Absorbed the Damage

The Philadelphia Semiconductor index fell 3.8% on Friday for an eighth consecutive loss, its longest streak since 2008. The iShares Semiconductor ETF fell about 4.1% for a ninth straight decline. That resolves the single test we carried into Friday, and it resolved against the market.

Beneath the index the damage was concentrated with precision. Neoclouds took the worst of it, with CoreWeave down 11.9% to $61.39, Nebius 12.2% and IREN 11.2%. CoreWeave is now down 25.2% on the week, its second double digit decline in three sessions. Memory and storage followed, with Seagate down 7.5%, SanDisk 6.8% and Micron 5.6%, on a combination of doubts about pricing power and continued progress reports from China's ChangXin Memory Technologies. Optical names were not spared, with Coherent down 7.1% and Ciena 6.7%. AMD fell 6.0% and Intel 5.8%.

Read that list carefully, because it is ordered. The names that fell hardest are the ones whose business models require external capital to convert demand into revenue. Neoclouds borrow to buy processors. Memory makers borrow to build fabs. Optical suppliers sit between the two. The names that held, Apple among them, fund their own capacity from operating cash. This is not a market rejecting artificial intelligence as a theme. It is a market applying a funding discount to the theme, company by company, in proportion to how much borrowed money each one needs.

Worth holding alongside that: Hock Tan of Broadcom and Lip-Bu Tan of Intel both said publicly on October 12 that capital, not chips or power, is likely to be the binding constraint on the build. Two of the three largest suppliers to this cycle told the market where the problem was five days before the market priced it. That is unusual, and it argues the repricing has further to run on fundamentals rather than merely on positioning.

## Credit: Still a Price Problem, Not Yet a Quantity Problem

Volta Infrastructure's $5bn package printed Thursday at 650 basis points over the benchmark at 95 cents, split between a $1.3bn term loan and a $3.7bn chip loan secured on Nvidia processors that the arrangers would only sell on a best efforts basis. We set a test on Friday that the loan should trade at or below 95 in the secondary market through the first week if the repricing is structural. No secondary quotes have surfaced, so that test stays open into next week rather than being quietly dropped.

CoreWeave's five year default insurance remains the cleanest live marker, having traded above 1,140 basis points before settling near 1,038, with the 2031 bond yielding close to 15.8%. Oracle's sits near 288 after a 26 basis point move, and Amazon, Alphabet and Meta have all seen record wides on their own protection while staying far below those levels.

Our framing from October 16 stands and we restate it without softening: this is a price constraint, not a quantity constraint. Volta's book cleared. Expensive capital is still capital. A failed book would force the neocloud build to be re-planned around equity, and nothing this week came close to that. What a price constraint does instead is compress the return on each dollar of capacity quarter after quarter, which shows up in equity multiples slowly and in the lenders' coupons immediately. Anybody positioning for a quantity constraint needs a book to fail, not to widen, and none has.

## Energy: Hormuz Has Become a Shipping Problem Rather Than a Headline

Brent settled at $109.86, up 3.7%, after touching $110 intraday for the first time since May. West Texas Intermediate settled at $101.41, up 3.5%. On the week WTI added more than 10% and Brent close to 5%. Energy was the best performing sector in a down tape, and diesel recovered the whole of the prior week's decline. Retail diesel averaged $6.33 a gallon on Friday, more than 70% above a year ago.

The reason the move is accelerating is that the risk has changed category. For months the Hormuz story was a sequence of headlines that pushed crude up and then faded as flows held. This week at least ten vessels were attacked in or near the strait, the highest weekly count since the conflict began in February, and flows ran at about 9.4m barrels a day for the week to Wednesday, roughly 70% of the pre-war average. This morning the Revolutionary Guard's navy said a tanker carrying more than 2m barrels struck a naval mine and caught fire while transiting an unauthorised route. That account is Iranian and unverified; neither US Central Command nor the UK Maritime Trade Operations has confirmed it, and a separate UKMTO report describes a tanker hit by a rocket propelled grenade from a small boat.

The detail that matters is not any single incident. It is that shipowners who accepted the risk of the chokepoint for eight months have begun rerouting. Once that decision is made it is slow to reverse, because it is an insurance and charter decision rather than a price decision. Add Ukraine's renewed strikes on Russian refineries, the collapse of the energy ceasefire, a congressional push to block the Russian diesel licence, and Houthi missiles reaching Aramco's Dhahran headquarters on Tuesday, and the supply side of this market has four independent sources of disruption running at once.

We have argued for a week that crude is a surplus story for 2027 and distillate a shortage story right now. That split held again, but the crude leg is getting harder to defend at $110. A 2027 surplus does not help a refiner who cannot get a cargo in November, and the freight and insurance costs now embedded in the delivered price are not going to unwind on an OPEC statement.

## Rates and the Fed: The Curve Declined to Join In

Chair Warsh spoke at the IMF meetings in Bangkok and gave no guidance, saying the committee had removed a dose of accommodation and that he would make his judgments at the October and December meetings. Traders read the silence as dovish. October 28 hike odds fell to about 18% from roughly 28%, while December stayed near 90%.

The curve did very little with any of it. The two year ended at 4.72%, down about 2 basis points, the ten year at 5.09%, up 2 to 3, and the thirty year at 5.43%, up 2. The ten year had reached 5.33% after Wednesday's consumer price report before reversing hard as money moved to safety.

This is the second consecutive session in which the front end refused to confirm the equity market's read, and we think the message is consistent. The two year is pricing a Fed that has nearly finished tightening and a labour market that is soft but not breaking. It is not pricing an AI credit event as a macro event. September industrial production supports that reading: output rose 0.7% against a 0.4% consensus, capacity utilisation improved to 76.9%, and semiconductor output grew 2.1% on the month. Strip out high technology and factory output rose only 0.2% and is up 0.7% over the year, which is the real shape of this economy. One narrow sector is doing the work, that sector is where the financing stress sits, and the rest is flat.

## Earnings That Matter: TSMC Confirmed the Demand and Flagged the Margin

The week's only print with real information for this market was Taiwan Semiconductor's on Wednesday. Everything else was banks and insurers.

| Company | Print | Guidance | Read |
|---------|-------|----------|------|
| TSMC | Q3 revenue $46.7bn, +41% y/y in dollars, gross margin 66.4% (-1.3pt q/q) | Q4 $52.3-54.3bn, FY26 revenue +44%, capex $60-64bn | Demand confirmed, margin is the new variable |
| Travelers | Core EPS $9.18 vs $7.07 consensus | None | Record investment income, the curve pays insurers |
| Alcoa | Adjusted $1.82 vs $1.54, revenue $3.7bn light | None | Plants beat, the metal price did not |

**TSMC** beat its own revenue guidance of $44.6bn to $45.8bn, exceeded its operating margin range, and guided the fourth quarter to $52.3bn to $54.3bn, which is roughly 14% sequential growth and close to 58% year on year. High performance computing rose 22% sequentially to 68% of revenue. Chief Executive C.C. Wei said demand remains far above what the company can supply. Capital expenditure was held at $60bn to $64bn. The shares fell anyway, on a 1.3 point sequential decline in gross margin driven by the 2 nanometre ramp and overseas fabs.

Our take is that this print settles the demand question and opens a different one. Nobody can look at a 58% growth guide from the company that builds nearly all of the world's leading edge logic and argue that compute demand is rolling over. What the market did with it instead is mark down the margin profile of the whole supply chain, and that is the correct instinct even if TSMC is the wrong target for it. TSMC has pricing power and a sold out book. Its customers, particularly the ones who borrow to buy capacity, have neither. The margin compression that showed up in TSMC's 2 nanometre ramp will show up much more painfully one layer down, in the neoclouds now paying 12% for chip loans and in memory makers facing a credible Chinese entrant.

**Travelers** earned core EPS of $9.18 against $7.07 expected, driven by record investment income, and closed up 1.5% at $389.54 after being down 4.0% early. The insurers were the quiet winners of this week, and the mechanism is the same curve that is costing the banks: a five handle on long Treasuries reprices an insurance float upward every quarter it persists. **Alcoa** beat at $1.82 adjusted against roughly $1.54 with revenue about 2% light, the upside coming from operations at Pinjarra rather than from the price deck, and generated $472m of free cash while funding its South32 acquisition.

## Asia and Europe Went the Other Way

Asia closed Friday higher almost everywhere. The Nikkei 225 rose 1.39% to 65,324.29 and the Topix added about 1.9%. The Kospi gained 2.05% to 5,668.05, its first advance in eight sessions after Thursday's 7.57% collapse, and traded as much as 4% higher intraday. The ASX 200 rose 1% to 8,806.70 and the Hang Seng closed up 0.39% at 24,157.33, while mainland China slipped into the red. MSCI Asia Pacific still finished with its worst week in more than three months.

Europe did better still. The Stoxx 600 rose 0.72% to 644.74 for its strongest week since late June, the DAX gained 0.95% to 25,622.61, the CAC 40 rose 0.28% to 7,956.70 and the FTSE 100 added 0.38% to 10,881.90.

The compositional argument explains most of this and we will keep making it until it stops working. These indices hold banks, insurers, defence, energy and luxury rather than memory, optics and neoclouds. They outperform when the problem is specific to leveraged American technology and they do not when the problem becomes the global cost of capital. Friday looked like the first again, and both Asia and Europe were trading a session behind the American close, so Monday's Asian open is the more informative data point than Friday's was.

## The Record

The ninth consecutive decline in the semiconductor ETF, which we flagged Friday as the thing to watch, happened. We take no credit for that. It was a continuation call on an eight session streak and the base rate did the work.

The call we will claim is the one we made almost as an aside. On Friday we wrote that the analytical centre of the market can be right and still be a minority of the realised volatility, and that when the biggest moves on the tape are produced by rulebooks and borrowed money the information content of price is lower than it looks. Friday's largest volatility event was an unconfirmed rumor about one fund's book. That is the same phenomenon we described, and it arrived within a session. We will also note the uncomfortable half of it, which is that we identified the mechanism while continuing to lead with credit for three straight notes. Describing a risk in the fifth paragraph is not the same as positioning for it.

The Volta secondary test stays open. No quotes have printed, and we would rather carry an unresolved test than quietly let it expire.

One new test for the coming week. If this is rotation rather than deleveraging, the equal weighted S&P 500 and the Dow should continue to hold or gain on days when the Nasdaq falls more than 1%. If we get a session where the Nasdaq falls more than 1% and the equal weighted index falls by a similar amount with volatility above 22, the leverage channel has taken over from the preference channel and our rotation framing is wrong.
