Tuesday, July 7, 2026, written pre-bell. Wall Street comes in this morning fresh off a strong reopening session, but the tape is already trying to take it back. On Monday the market roared out of the July 4 break with a chip-led rally that pushed the Dow to a record 53,055.91, lifted the S&P 500 by 0.72 percent to 7,537.43, and drove the Nasdaq Composite up 1.12 percent to 26,121 as faith in the AI trade came flooding back. Twelve hours later that faith is wobbling again. Nasdaq 100 futures are down about 1 percent, with Micron, Broadcom, AMD, Marvell, and KLA all lower before the bell, while Dow futures point up roughly 225 points and the S&P sits just below flat. Two large events sit on top of the whipsaw. SpaceX officially joins the Nasdaq-100 at the open, unleashing billions in forced passive buying, and overnight a suspected Iranian missile set a Qatari LNG tanker ablaze in the Strait of Hormuz, the first attack on a gas carrier since the war began. The setup into the open is a market that cannot decide whether the AI leaders are a buy or a source of funds, layered on top of a geopolitical tail that just reappeared.

## Top Call: The Memory Whipsaw Is the Tell, Not the Rally

The one thing PMs need to carry into the open is that Monday's relief rally already failed to hold for a full session, and that failure matters more than the bounce did. A complex that ripped on Friday, ripped again Monday, and is being dumped for the third time this week before Tuesday's bell is not trading on fundamentals in either direction. The cleanest proof arrived overnight from Samsung, which pre-announced an eighteen-fold jump in second-quarter operating profit and still sold off, dragging the Kospi down roughly 5 percent. When the single best earnings signal in the group produces a lower stock, you are watching positioning, not thesis. Our read is unchanged from last week. Stay long the genuinely scarce parts of the buildout, high-bandwidth memory and high-end storage, and treat the daily lurches as the price of admission in a crowded trade that has not finished shaking out weak hands. The more interesting money is in the rotation underneath: the Dow keeps printing records on financials and value while the Nasdaq convulses, and that breadth, not the chip bounce, is the healthiest feature of this market. On top of it all, respect the Hormuz headline. Oil had been left for dead on an emerging glut, and a live attack on a gas tanker is exactly the kind of shock a complacent crude market is unprepared for.

## SpaceX Joins the Nasdaq-100: Forced Buying Into a Sliver of Float

The marquee event today is mechanical, not fundamental. SpaceX enters the Nasdaq-100 at the open under the exchange's new fast-track rule that lets a new listing qualify on its fifteenth trading day, and it goes in at a capitalization set to three times its raw float, giving it an index weight near 1.3 percent on a valuation that now tops 2 trillion dollars. Because only 3 to 5 percent of the shares actually trade, the passive demand is enormous relative to available stock. JPMorgan pegs roughly 4.3 billion dollars of buying from QQQ and QQQM alone, and once you add every other benchmark and fund that has to track the index the forced total climbs toward 27 billion dollars, all of it hitting a name with a tiny public float.

Our take is to respect the flow but not to chase it. History is unkind to index additions once the mechanical bid clears. The average Nasdaq-100 entrant slips about 1.1 percent on its first day and roughly 3.4 percent over its first five sessions, as the forced buyers finish and the market reprices the stock on its own merits. Layer in the lockup expirations that will let SpaceX insiders sell over the coming weeks, and the setup is a squeeze into a supply cliff. The stock changed hands near 156 to 162 dollars ahead of the debut, and the honest posture is that a float this thin can trade far above fair value today and give a chunk of it back once the passive engine goes quiet. This is a flow event to trade around, not a fundamental entry point.

## Chips Sell Off Again: Samsung's Blowout Cannot Hold the Group

The clearest sign the memory complex has not stabilized is that Samsung pre-announced a monster quarter overnight and the stock still fell. The company guided to an operating profit up roughly eighteen times year over year, powered by AI memory demand, and the reaction was a drop of about 7 percent that took the Kospi down near 5 percent and pulled US chip futures with it. Micron, Broadcom, AMD, Marvell, and KLA are all lower premarket, unwinding a good part of Monday's chip-led pop. The proximate trigger for the renewed caution is the same one that started the late-June rout, a guarded AI outlook from Broadcom paired with fears that the data-center buildout slows if hyperscalers throttle spending, now compounded by memory oversupply worries as Korean capacity comes online.

Our read is that this remains a positioning story rather than a broken thesis, and Samsung's number is the evidence. Demand is not the problem when the largest memory maker in the world is guiding to an eighteen-fold profit jump. The problem is that this group became the most crowded long on the Street at rich multiples, and crowded longs get sold first and hardest on any excuse. We stay constructive on the pricing power in high-bandwidth memory and cautious on the parts of compute that Meta Compute's capacity-rental reframe suggested could commoditize. The one real overhang to watch is Friday, when SK Hynix drops its roughly 29 billion dollar Nasdaq listing into the exact group that spent all week fighting over supply. Fresh mega-cap memory paper into a jittery tape can pull capital straight out of the incumbents.

## The Rotation: A Dow Record While the Nasdaq Convulses

What keeps this from being a simple risk-off morning is the breadth beneath the surface. The Dow closed at a record 53,055.91 on Monday and futures point higher again today even as Nasdaq futures fall, the signature of a market rotating out of crowded AI winners and into financials, defensives, and rate-sensitive value it had left behind. This is the same rotation the July 2 jobs miss set in motion, and it has quietly broadened leadership down the cap scale and across sectors while the megacap complex chops sideways.

Our take is that this broadening is the most durable thing in the tape. A market led only by a handful of chip and platform names is fragile, and the past week has spread the leadership out. The caution is that the catalyst powering the breadth, a labor market cooling fast enough to revive cut hopes, is double-edged. If the coming data confirm a soft landing, the Dow-led rotation has real legs. If they confirm hiring has stalled, the cyclicals and small caps that lead on the way up are the first to roll over. For now, own the record-setting breadth and let the Nasdaq sort itself out.

## Hormuz Reawakens: A Gas Tanker Struck, Oil Snaps Higher

The overnight geopolitical shock is the one the market least expected. The Qatari LNG carrier Al Rekayyat was struck in the early hours of Tuesday about 8 nautical miles east of Limah, Oman, and set ablaze while exiting the Strait of Hormuz, with security consultancies classifying it as a drone or missile strike and Iranian state media implying Tehran was behind it. Reports cited at least two missiles fired by Iran's Revolutionary Guards at commercial ships transiting the strait overnight. This is the first LNG tanker hit since the war began, and it lands directly on the fragile US-Iran understanding that was supposed to keep the waterway calm while indirect talks continued.

Crude snapped higher on the news, with WTI bouncing above 69 dollars to a one-week high and Brent back above 72, but both remain close to their lowest levels since late February. That tension is the whole story in oil right now. The bearish structural force is real and getting stronger. OPEC+ agreed over the weekend to raise quotas by another 188,000 barrels a day, Iranian barrels keep flooding back, and Russian exports stay near records, all of which had crude pricing an emerging glut and almost none of the war premium it carried in the spring. Our take is that the market has grown dangerously complacent on Hormuz. A single tanker strike is a headline; a pattern of them would force shippers to reroute or demand war-risk premiums, and a crude market this short of risk premium would have to reprice quickly. We would not fade the oil bounce here, and we would treat energy as the cheapest hedge against a chokepoint that just proved it is not as quiet as the price implied.

## Macro: The Minutes Meet a Jobs Miss

The macro focus this week is Wednesday's June FOMC minutes, and the interesting part is the collision they will document. That June meeting was Kevin Warsh's first as chair, and the committee held at 3.5 to 3.75 percent while turning hawkish, stripping its easing bias, and moving the median dot to signal at least one hike this year, with nine of eighteen members penciling in hikes against eight for no change and just one for a cut. Then, days after the meeting, the June payrolls report landed at just 57,000 jobs against expectations near 115,000, with prior months revised down a combined 74,000 and participation at a multi-year low. The market resolved that clash in favor of the weak data, pricing a September cut back into the curve, pulling the two-year toward 4.02 percent and the ten-year to about 4.35 percent, and softening the dollar off its highs.

Our read is that the minutes are backward-looking relative to that jobs number, so the market may discount a hawkish tone as stale. What matters is whether the coming hard data confirm the labor-market cooling that put cuts back on the table. This morning's calendar is quiet after Monday's ISM services print, and the events that actually move this tape start next week, when the big banks open second-quarter season on July 14 alongside June CPI. Until then, a market rallying on a soft jobs number into a week dominated by index flows and a fresh geopolitical shock is running on liquidity and positioning, which can carry further than it should and unwind fast when the fundamentals arrive.

## Asia and Europe: A Split Tape

Asia traded in two directions overnight. South Korea's Kospi fell roughly 5 percent as Samsung's blowout guidance failed to hold and the memory names rolled over again, making Korea the epicenter of the chip unwind for the second time in a week. Mainland China was heavy for a different reason, with the Shanghai Composite sliding 1.26 percent to 3,990 and the CSI 300 down about 1 percent as worries over the domestic growth outlook resurfaced, both benchmarks settling near one-month lows. Hong Kong bucked the trend, with the Hang Seng up about 1.1 percent to 23,616, and Japan's Nikkei 225 held near a record with its broad leadership carrying a year-to-date gain around 38 percent that has let it sidestep the worst of the memory panic.

Europe opened cautious and mixed after Monday's session, when the Stoxx 600 slipped 0.4 percent to 650 even as the FTSE 100, DAX, and CAC 40 all closed higher. The regional benchmarks were seen opening roughly flat to slightly lower this morning, with only the DAX pointed marginally green. The picture into the New York open is a split global tape, memory-driven weakness in Korea and growth worries in China against firmer prints in Hong Kong, Japan, and a steady Europe, all of it framed by the Hormuz headline and the resumption of the chip selloff.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran:** Overnight's tanker strike is the sharpest test yet of the fragile understanding meant to keep Hormuz open. The Qatari LNG carrier Al Rekayyat was hit and set ablaze near Oman as it exited the strait, with Iranian state media implying responsibility and no formal claim. Indirect talks through Qatari and Pakistani mediators were already on pause until funeral rites for Iran's former supreme leader conclude July 9, and an attack during that window raises the odds the next technical round slips or hardens. The strait stays open and crude still prices calm, but this is the largest swing factor left for oil and it just moved the wrong way.

**Ukraine-Russia:** Structurally unresolved and overshadowed by the Gulf. Combat continues along the eastern axes, a pending US security-assistance package remains the live source of fresh headline risk, and record Russian oil exports quietly reinforce the global crude glut that has capped prices all spring.

**Taiwan Strait and South China Sea:** Low-simmer coast guard pressure continues with no major escalation, a contained pattern that stays in the background for now.
