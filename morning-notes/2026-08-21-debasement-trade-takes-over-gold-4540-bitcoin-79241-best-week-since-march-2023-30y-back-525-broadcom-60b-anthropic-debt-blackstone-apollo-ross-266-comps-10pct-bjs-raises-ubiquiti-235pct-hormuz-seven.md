Friday, August 21, 2026, written at 9:15am Eastern, fifteen minutes before the bell, with the flash PMIs still half an hour away and Bitcoin having printed $79,241 overnight. Yesterday this note argued that the dollar had become the cleanest expression of the whole setup, and that gold and Bitcoin were the same trade wearing different clothes. That has been the right frame, though not in the way we drew the line. The dollar itself did not break 98.50. It sat at 98.802 and did nothing. What moved instead were the two assets that have no issuer, and they moved with real violence.

## Top Call: The Buyback Did Not Rescue the Bond, It Repriced the Money

Three days ago the Treasury doubled the size of its long-dated buyback operations to at least $4 billion per operation, and the 30-year fell nine basis points. This morning the 30-year is back at 5.25% and the 10-year is at 4.70%, up from 4.64% on Wednesday. Every basis point of relief has been returned. The intervention bought one session, which is what we said yesterday, and now it has bought less than that.

But look at what did keep the gains. Gold is at $4,540.18 spot, up 3.6% on the week and heading for a third consecutive weekly advance, with the futures contract up 1.38% at $4,634.60. Bitcoin has climbed for five straight sessions, added roughly 24% since Monday morning, touched $79,241 overnight for its highest level since June, and is now trading near $77,184. That is the strongest weekly move in the asset since March 2023. About $3 billion of shorts have been run in, and US spot Bitcoin ETFs took $606 million on Thursday after $517 million on Wednesday, so this is not purely a squeeze any more. Real money is following the liquidation.

That combination is the whole story and it deserves to be stated plainly. When a central bank or a fiscal authority announces that it will buy its own long-dated debt, there are two possible market readings. The first is that the buyer is a stabiliser and the bond should rally. The second is that the buyer is a monetiser and the currency should fall. The market ran the first interpretation for about six hours on Wednesday and has spent every hour since running the second. Gold, Bitcoin and a dollar stuck at a three-month low are all the same position. The bond is the asset that told you the first reading failed.

The equity market has not fully processed this. Futures are firm, with the S&P up 0.50% at 7,700.75, the Nasdaq 100 up 0.72% and on track to snap a five-session losing streak, and the Russell 2000 up 0.81%. The VIX is at 15.43, down another 3.62%. Stocks are treating a stabilising yield as good news. Our read is that yields have not stabilised, they have simply stopped rising for a morning, and the assets that price the alternative to fiat have been telling a much more consistent story all week.

## Broadcom: The AI Buildout Becomes a Credit Market Event

The most important corporate story of the last twenty-four hours has almost nothing to do with chips and everything to do with the bond market that we just spent three paragraphs discussing. Bloomberg reported Thursday evening that Broadcom is in talks with lenders to raise more than $60 billion of debt to finance AI chip capacity that will benefit Anthropic and other customers. The structure under discussion is large and unusual. There is a junior tranche of roughly $30 billion, a senior secured tranche in the range of $60 billion to $70 billion that Broadcom would partly guarantee, and a total package that people close to the talks put as high as $100 billion. Blackstone and Apollo are both at the table, building on a three-way partnership the firms struck in June. Broadcom shares are up more than 1% pre-market.

| Broadcom AI financing | Reported terms | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Total package | up to about $100B | among the largest ever assembled |
| Junior tranche | roughly $30B | private credit takes the risk seam |
| Senior secured | about $60B to $70B | partly guaranteed by Broadcom |
| Lenders in talks | Blackstone, Apollo | extends the June partnership |
| Beneficiary | Anthropic and others | on top of a prior $21B commitment |

Set this beside what we already knew. Anthropic has committed roughly $21 billion to custom silicon through Broadcom, has secured close to a million Google TPU v7p units for delivery by late this year, and has contracted for 3.5 gigawatts of TPU capacity beginning in 2027. Anthropic also reported a June quarter at roughly $11.5 billion of annualised revenue with its first operating profit, which this note covered on Monday. So the demand side of this financing is not speculative in the way that the 2000 telecom buildouts were. There is a customer, there is revenue, and there is a signed capacity commitment.

The issue is the location of the funding, not the quality of the borrower. On Wednesday a Wall Street Journal tally put roughly $3 trillion of AI infrastructure commitments off the balance sheets of nine companies against about $600 billion of reported capital expenditure. Broadcom's package is a further instalment of exactly that structure, and it is being arranged in the same credit market that the Treasury is currently trying to steady by buying back its own long bonds. When the fiscal authority is a buyer of duration at $4 billion an operation, and the private sector is simultaneously seeking $100 billion of new duration to build data centres, the two are competing for the same pool of capital. That competition is a large part of why a 5.25% thirty-year yield refuses to come down on a buyback announcement. The bond market is not only pricing deficits. It is pricing the AI buildout as a claim on savings.

We would also note the ownership of the risk. The junior tranche sits with private credit, and Blackstone and Apollo are the named participants. That is a transfer of AI capacity risk from public equity holders, who can mark it daily, to private vehicles, where the mark is quarterly and modelled. It is a good outcome for Broadcom's cost of capital and a worse outcome for the market's ability to see stress early if the compute demand curve ever bends.

## The Debasement Complex

Gold's rally is now well past the point where it can be explained by geopolitical hedging. Spot at $4,540.18 is up 3.6% this week and going for a third straight weekly gain, and the jump of more than 4% on Wednesday came within hours of the Treasury buyback headline rather than any Middle East development. Prediction markets have moved with it, with the odds of gold trading $4,700 during August rising to about 44% from 22% in a single day. Citi's Dirk Willer has been arguing that the renewed debasement trade opens a path to $5,000 to $6,000 over the coming year. We do not need to endorse the target to accept the mechanism, which is that a currency with a fiscal authority openly purchasing its own long debt trades at a lower real value against hard assets.

Bitcoin has the same driver plus a policy catalyst of its own. The President used a White House meeting with industry executives to press the Senate to pass the stalled Digital Asset Market Clarity Act, and that regulatory clarity is what turned a bond-driven liquidity impulse into a five-day trend. The ETF flows are the part worth watching. Two consecutive days of accelerating inflows, $517 million and then $606 million, suggest allocators rather than leveraged accounts are now doing the buying, which typically makes the move stickier than a pure squeeze but also sets up a sharper reversal if the macro impulse fades.

The dollar is the piece that has not confirmed. At 98.802 it is down about 0.9% on the week and pinned near a three-month low, with the yen firmer at 158.70 from 159.05 and the euro at 1.1702 from 1.1678. We flagged 98.50 yesterday as the level that would matter. It has not broken. So the honest read is that the debasement trade is being expressed in gold and Bitcoin, which are unencumbered, rather than in the dollar, which still has the highest policy rate in the developed world holding it up. That tension is unstable. Either the metals and crypto are running ahead of the currency and will correct, or the dollar is lagging and 98.50 goes. We lean toward the second because the front end has already stopped believing in a hike, with the two-year at 4.12% and September odds near 30%.

## Data and the Fed: Flash PMIs Into Warsh's First Jackson Hole

The only US data of consequence today is the S&P Global flash composite at 9:45am Eastern, against a prior composite reading of 54.5. July's detail matters more than the headline will. Services came in at 54.6, the sharpest expansion in nine months, manufacturing at 53.8, and input cost inflation hit a fourteen-month high on tariffs and raw materials while export orders fell the most since 2022. If today's release repeats that combination of firm activity and hot input costs, it argues against the market's roughly 70% probability of a September pause, and it argues for exactly the fiscal and inflation anxiety that has the long end stuck above 5.2%.

Next week is the event. The Kansas City Fed's symposium runs August 27 to 29 at Jackson Lake Lodge, and Kevin Warsh delivers the keynote on the morning of Friday the 28th, his first Jackson Hole address as chair. His communication approach since taking over has been described as clear objectives with an ambiguous path, which has served him reasonably well in a year with an oil shock, but it will be tested by a market that is now openly trading the fiscal authority rather than the monetary one. The question the audience will actually want answered is not whether September brings a hike. It is what the Federal Reserve thinks about a Treasury that is buying its own thirty-year paper while gold and Bitcoin go vertical.

## Oil and Iran: Seven Crossings and a Monday Deadline

Brent is at $93.67, essentially unchanged on the day and up more than 5% on the week. WTI is $86.77. The weekly move is entirely a supply and policy story, because the demand forecasts have gone the other way. Both OPEC and the International Energy Agency cut their 2026 demand outlooks, with the IEA now expecting world oil demand to fall 1.6 million barrels a day this year, a downgrade of 510,000 barrels from the prior month, attributing it directly to the continued closure of the Strait of Hormuz and elevated fuel prices. A market that rallies 5% in a week while the two principal forecasting bodies slash demand is a market where supply risk dominates completely.

Thursday brought the escalation. Crossings through the Strait fell to seven from fourteen the day before. Treasury Secretary Bessent told CNBC that "you are either with us, or against us" and promised "the greatest coordinated economic isolation in the history of the world," with a press conference scheduled for Monday to detail the measures. The stated design is a combination of secondary sanctions on Iran's economic partners and a continued naval blockade that keeps cargo from moving in or out of Iranian ports. Iranian parliament speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf publicly warned that military strength cannot preserve the Republic if the population goes hungry and the economy fails to grow, which is the clearest sign of internal strain we have seen from Tehran in this conflict.

The risk nobody has priced sits in Beijing. China is the largest buyer of Iranian crude, and a genuinely coordinated secondary sanctions regime necessarily targets Chinese refiners and Chinese banks. Bloomberg has already framed Monday's announcement as carrying blowback risk with China. For a market that has spent two years assuming the US and China would keep trade and security disputes in separate boxes, Monday is the day that assumption gets tested. If the measures reach Chinese entities, the second-order effect lands on Treasury demand and on the semiconductor supply chain, not on crude. We would rather have that risk on the calendar than as a surprise, and it is now on the calendar.

## Asia and Europe

Asia was mostly firm and notably calm compared with the two extraordinary sessions that preceded it. The Nikkei slipped 0.3% to 66,016.36 as Japan's July headline inflation rose to 1.9%, with the acceleration attributed partly to elevated oil prices from the Iran conflict. That number keeps the Bank of Japan's normalisation live and the ten-year JGB near the three-decade highs it reached on Tuesday. Korea's Kospi added 0.9% to 6,912.95, a third of the way to recovering the round trip it has made this week between a 5.80% loss and a 5.89% gain. Hong Kong was the best performer, with the Hang Seng up 1.2% to 26,009.46, and the Shanghai Composite added a fraction to 3,905.20 with the CSI 300 roughly flat.

Europe is quiet and slightly better bid, with the DAX up 0.1% at 26,015.14, the FTSE 100 up 0.1% at 10,752.29 and the CAC 40 close to unchanged at 8,454.20. The number that matters is the Stoxx 600 at 650.35, which closed lower for a seventh consecutive session on Thursday, its longest losing streak since September 2023. Europe hit a record on August 5 and has spent the two weeks since giving it back, and the proximate cause is the same oil-driven inflation anxiety that has the long end of every developed market under pressure. A European index that cannot rally on a weaker dollar is telling you the energy input is doing more work than the currency.

## What Matters From Here

Own the scorecard first. Yesterday we said the long end would struggle to hold 5.25% into next week's supply and that the intervention would decay. It has decayed, and the 30-year is at 5.25%, which is the line we drew. We also said the dollar was the cleanest expression of the setup and put the level at 98.50. That has not worked. The dollar has gone sideways at 98.80 and the move has expressed itself entirely in gold and Bitcoin instead. The direction was right and the instrument was wrong, and the reason is one we should have weighted more heavily. A currency with the highest policy rate in the G10 does not fall easily even when its issuer is monetising, because the carry keeps buyers in. Gold and Bitcoin have no carry to defend and so they move first.

That reframes what we are watching now. The first thing is whether the flash PMI at 9:45 confirms July's pattern of firm activity with fourteen-month-high input costs. If it does, the front end at 4.12% and September pause odds near 70% are both too complacent, and the pressure returns to the long end immediately rather than waiting for next week's supply.

The second is Broadcom's book building. A $60 billion to $100 billion package is large enough that its pricing becomes a market event in its own right. If it clears easily, the AI buildout has demonstrated it can fund itself in scale without equity, which is supportive for the semiconductor complex and unhelpful for Treasury yields. If it prices wide or shrinks, that is the first real evidence that the credit market has a limit on this theme, and it would land four days before Nvidia reports.

The third is Monday. Bessent has pre-announced a press conference and used language about coordinated economic isolation that leaves very little room to underdeliver. The market has priced the oil consequence, with Brent above $93 and crossings down to seven. It has not priced a version of these measures that reaches Chinese refiners and Chinese banks. Between now and the open on Monday, that is the largest identifiable gap between what is scheduled and what is discounted.

All marks above are pre-market and will move by the open.
