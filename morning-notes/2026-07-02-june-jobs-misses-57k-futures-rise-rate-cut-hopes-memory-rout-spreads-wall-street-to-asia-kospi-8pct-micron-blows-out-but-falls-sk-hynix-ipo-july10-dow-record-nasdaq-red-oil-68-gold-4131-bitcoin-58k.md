Thursday, July 2, 2026, written just after the 8:30 payrolls print in a holiday-shortened week, and the number everyone had circled landed with a thud. The June jobs report showed the economy adding only 57,000 positions against a 113,000 estimate, the weakest read in months and the third soft labor print in a row after ADP's 98,000 miss. Yet the unemployment rate slipped to 4.2 percent rather than rising, and the market's first instinct was relief, not fear. Futures turned green on the read that a cooling labor market forces the Fed's hand back toward cuts, with small caps out front, the VIX falling toward 16, gold jumping past 4,130, and Treasury yields sliding across the curve. Underneath that tidy headline, though, the tape is anything but calm. A memory-chip rout that started on Wall Street on Wednesday has swept through Asia overnight, and the very trade that powered the best quarter since 2020 is coming apart at exactly the moment the index turns higher.

## Top Call: A Dovish Jobs Shock Masks a Leadership Change

The one thing PMs need to carry into the open is that the green screen this morning is hiding a rotation, not confirming the old trend. Yes, a 57,000 payrolls print is dovish, and yes it revives the September cut the tape had priced out after Warsh turned hawkish. But look at what is leading and what is bleeding. The Russell is up more than twice the Nasdaq, the Dow closed at a record on Wednesday while the Nasdaq fell over a percent, and the memory and storage names that carried the entire second quarter are the epicenter of a selloff that just crushed the Kospi by nearly 8 percent overnight. This is the market taking the rate-cut gift and using it to fund a move out of the crowded AI winners and into the rate-sensitive laggards it left behind. Our read is to respect the rotation and stop treating memory as a one-way trade. We flagged this complex as the highest-quality way to stay long AI as recently as yesterday, and we were early to the crack, so we will own that: the pricing-power story is intact but the positioning got far too crowded, and a blowout quarter from Micron that the stock ignored is the tell. Lean into the broadening, not the names that already ran.

## The Memory Rout: Micron Blows Out and Falls Anyway

The clearest sign the leadership is changing is that the best fundamental news of the week produced the worst stock reaction. Micron reported a monster fiscal third quarter, with revenue of 41.46 billion dollars against a 35.69 billion consensus and adjusted earnings of 25.11 dollars a share versus 20.49 expected, a beat of almost any measure you want to use. And the stock fell more than 9 percent on Wednesday, sliding under 1,000 dollars even after a 260 percent run this year. When a print that good gets sold that hard, the tape is telling you the good news was already in the price and the marginal buyer has left.

Three things turned the fundamentals into a selloff. First, a federal price-fixing suit filed in late June accuses Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix of colluding to keep DRAM artificially scarce and lifting prices roughly 700 percent over four years, which puts a regulatory cloud over the exact pricing power that made the trade work. Second, planned capacity expansions in Korea have revived the oldest fear in memory, an oversupply cycle, right as demand signals soften. Third, and most immediate, SK Hynix has set a 29.4 billion dollar Nasdaq listing to begin trading July 10, and the market is worried that a fresh mega-cap memory name will pull capital straight out of the incumbents. Our take is that this is a positioning unwind rather than a thesis break. The AI capacity crunch is real and Micron's numbers prove it, but the group became a consensus long at rich multiples, and consensus longs are precisely what get sold first when the crowd reaches for an exit.

## Asia Contagion: The Kospi Cracks and China Tech Follows

The overnight session showed how fast a US chip wobble becomes a global one when a single sector dominates an index. Korea took the brunt of it. The Kospi fell close to 8 percent, its worst session in weeks, because Samsung and SK Hynix now make up roughly half the index by weight, up from about a quarter at the end of last year, so a memory selloff is effectively a selloff of the whole market. Samsung dropped around 9 percent and SK Hynix fell even harder into its listing. The damage spread across the region's chip supply chain, with China's largest foundry SMIC down more than 11 percent in Hong Kong and Hua Hong off around 14 percent, dragging Hong Kong and mainland tech lower.

Japan was the exception that proves the point. The Nikkei actually rose about 0.9 percent to 70,671 because its leadership is broader and less concentrated in memory, so the same macro tailwind that lifted US small caps helped Tokyo while it did nothing for Seoul. The lesson for the book is concentration risk. When two names are half your index, you do not own a market, you own a pair trade, and that is a warning worth carrying into any AI-heavy allocation right now.

## Macro: The Labor Market Buckles as the Fed Talks Tough

The tension that has defined this market all week finally snapped into the open with the jobs number. A 57,000 print is not just soft, it is the kind of read that changes the growth conversation, and it lands directly against a Fed that has spent the past two weeks leaning hawkish. New Chair Kevin Warsh said flatly at the Sintra forum that inflation is still too high, and nine of his eighteen colleagues have signaled support for higher rates this year, six of them for two hikes. That collision is the whole story. The data says the economy is cooling fast enough to demand cuts, and the central bank says prices will not let it deliver them. The market resolved that tension this morning in favor of the data, pricing a September cut back in, pulling the two-year down toward 4.02 percent and the ten-year to about 4.36 percent, and knocking the dollar lower. The clean read is dovish and the tape believed it. The messier read is that a labor market adding under 60,000 jobs while the Fed frets about inflation is the exact stagflation-lite setup that punishes long-duration winners, which is another reason the AI leaders are not the place to hide even as rate-cut odds climb.

## Oil: Below $68 and Still Doing the Fed's Work

Crude keeps grinding lower and it remains the friendliest crosscurrent in the whole picture. WTI is off another 1.2 percent this morning to 67.74 and Brent is near 71, extending a slide that has taken crude down almost 30 percent over the past month. The driver is unchanged and structural. Iran says it has shipped more than 40 million barrels since the naval blockade came off, Russian exports are running at record levels, OPEC keeps adding supply, and the market no longer prices any real risk of a Hormuz closure, so analysts are now openly warning about a glut in the back half of the year. For portfolios this is straightforward disinflation. Cheap energy feeds directly into the numbers the Fed watches and reinforces the rate-cut case that a soft jobs report just strengthened. The only caution worth naming is the same one as yesterday: oil this weak is partly a peace-and-supply story and partly a demand story, and a market cheering the disinflation should at least ask whether crude is also flagging that growth is cooling faster than record-adjacent equities assume.

## Iran: Talks Grind On in Doha and the Strait Stays Open

The geopolitical file stayed quiet enough to keep pressuring oil rather than supporting it. Indirect technical talks between Washington and Tehran are continuing in Doha with Qatar and Pakistan mediating, and the two sides agreed to keep the process going after meeting separately this week, building on the 60-day memorandum the presidents signed in mid-June. Kpler counted 34 verified vessel crossings through the Strait of Hormuz on the latest read, so shipping lanes are operationally open even if routing has not fully normalized. The friction point is money, not missiles. Iran keeps insisting it will charge service fees for passage rather than guarantee toll-free transit, which is leverage dressed up as logistics. Our read is unchanged: open lanes are the correct base case because that is the only variable that moves the real economy, but the deal is a framework, not a signature, and a breakdown before the 60-day window closes is still the cheapest tail nobody wants to pay for with the VIX at 16.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran:** Indirect technical talks continue in Doha through Qatari and Pakistani mediators, with both sides agreeing to keep going and the Strait of Hormuz operationally open. The sticking point is Iran's demand for passage service fees rather than the toll-free transit the June memorandum called for. This remains the single largest swing factor for oil and gold, and the framework is still unsigned.

**Ukraine-Russia:** Structurally unresolved and overshadowed by the Gulf, with Russian crude exports at record levels feeding directly into the global supply glut now pressuring oil. Combat continues along the eastern axes and a pending US security package is the live headline risk.

**Taiwan Strait and South China Sea:** PRC coast guard pressure near the Pratas Islands continues at a low simmer with no major escalation. The pattern of incremental activity persists but stays contained.
