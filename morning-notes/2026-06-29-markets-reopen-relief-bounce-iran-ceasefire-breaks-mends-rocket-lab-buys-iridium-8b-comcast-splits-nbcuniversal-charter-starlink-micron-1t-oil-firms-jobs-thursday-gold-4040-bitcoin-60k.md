Monday, June 29, 2026, written pre-bell. US equity and bond markets reopen this morning for the first time since Friday, and they reopen into a weekend that managed to be both more dangerous and more hopeful than the one that preceded it. The Iran ceasefire cracked open on Saturday and Sunday, with the US striking five Iranian coastal sites and Iran answering with drones and ballistic missiles aimed at American bases in Kuwait and Bahrain and at targets in the UAE, before both governments stepped back late Sunday and agreed to stand down and meet in Doha this week. Markets are reading the de-escalation, not the escalation. S&P 500 futures are up about 1.0 percent, Nasdaq 100 futures lead at roughly 1.3 percent, and the Dow is the laggard at about 0.4 percent, a clean reversal of last week's pattern in which the most expensive technology fell the hardest. The setup into the open is a risk-on bounce layered on top of a still-live geopolitical tail and a hawkish Fed, and the corporate tape is doing a lot of the lifting.

## Top Call: A Relief Rally Built on Dealmaking, Not an All-Clear

The single thing PMs need to carry into the open is that this is a relief bounce with real corporate fuel behind it, not a resolution of anything that drove last week's selloff. Three things turned the tape green at once. Iran and the US pulled back from a genuine weekend exchange of fire and put talks back on the calendar. Tech is bouncing after its worst week since spring. And the M&A engine roared back to life with Rocket Lab's 8 billion dollar bid for Iridium and Comcast's decision to break itself in two. None of that repairs the two real problems hanging over the market, which are an AI complex still being repriced on how its build-out gets financed and a Fed that has turned openly hawkish into a hot inflation print. Our read is to respect the bounce and fade the urge to chase it. The deals are a sign that strategic buyers see value at these levels, which is constructive, but the index is rallying on takeover premiums and a war that paused rather than ended, and Thursday's jobs report can undo a lot of this in a single number. We stay constructive on quality and cash flow and treat today's gap higher as a chance to upgrade, not to add beta.

## The Space Trade Gets a Champion: Rocket Lab Buys Iridium for $8B

The most important headline of the morning is the boldest. Rocket Lab is acquiring Iridium for about 8 billion dollars in enterprise value, paying 54 dollars a share in a cash-and-stock structure of 27 dollars cash plus stock, and funding the cash leg with balance-sheet money and a 3.6 billion dollar bridge loan. Iridium is up roughly 22 percent pre-market toward the deal price and Rocket Lab is up around 9 to 11 percent, which is the tell that matters. The market is rewarding the acquirer, not punishing it, even with a sizable bridge loan attached. That is a vote of confidence in the strategy. Rocket Lab is buying a working global satellite-communications network, its spectrum, and a 500-plus partner ecosystem, and bolting it onto its own launch and satellite-manufacturing business to create a vertically integrated company that designs, builds, launches, and operates its own constellations.

The strategic logic writes itself once you say it out loud. This is the first credible attempt to build a public-market answer to SpaceX and Starlink, combining the rocket, the factory, and the network under one roof. SpaceX has spent the month being the most-watched name in the market, round-tripping its blockbuster IPO and now joining the Nasdaq-100 on July 7, and Rocket Lab is effectively planting a flag as the alternative the public markets can actually own outright. Our take is that the space theme just graduated from a single dominant private name to a genuine investable group, and that is healthy for the sector even if the deal math gives some investors pause. The risk is the balance sheet. A 3.6 billion dollar bridge against a company Rocket Lab's size is real leverage in a market that spent all of last week worrying about exactly that, debt-funded ambition in the most speculative corners. The close is not slated until mid-2027, so this is a story stock and a thesis, not a near-term earnings event, but it reframes the entire space and satellite complex this morning.

## Comcast Breaks in Two and Charter Pops on a Starlink Angle

The other deal driving the tape is Comcast's decision to split into two public companies, and it is up about 22 to 25 percent on the news, an enormous move for a company this size. The plan spins NBCUniversal and Sky, including the Universal theme parks and the NBC and Sky networks, into a standalone media and entertainment business, while the legacy Comcast keeps the connectivity engine of Xfinity broadband, Xfinity Wireless, and Comcast Business. Co-CEO Mike Cavanagh takes the helm at NBCUniversal, former CFO Michael Angelakis becomes CEO of the remaining Comcast, and Brian Roberts stays chairman across both. Comcast keeps a stake of up to 19.9 percent in the spun media company for up to a year, and the whole thing is targeted to close in roughly twelve months pending the usual approvals.

The market reaction is really a verdict on conglomerate discounts. Investors have wanted the cable-and-connectivity cash machine separated from the lower-margin, structurally challenged media assets for years, and they are paying up the moment management finally agrees to do it. The more interesting second-order move is Charter, up around 20 percent, lifted both by the read-through that broadband connectivity is worth more on its own and by reports it is in talks with SpaceX, presumably to weave Starlink into its offering. That is the thread connecting this morning's two biggest deals. The same satellite-broadband future that makes Rocket Lab buy Iridium is what makes a cable operator want a Starlink partnership, and the terrestrial connectivity names are suddenly being valued on whether they have a space strategy. Our read is that the media-and-telecom complex is entering a breakup-and-realignment phase, and the spread between owners of scarce connectivity assets and owners of commoditized content is set to widen.

## Semis and AI: A Rebound With Micron Past $1 Trillion

The chips are leading the bounce, which is the cleanest sign that this is a risk-on session rather than a defensive one. The group is recovering after a brutal week, and the standout fundamental marker is Micron crossing a 1 trillion dollar market capitalization for the first time, joining Nvidia, Broadcom, Taiwan Semiconductor, and Samsung in the trillion-dollar club on the back of its record memory quarter and a guide that points sharply higher again. That milestone lands at an awkward but instructive moment. Last week the market sold Micron's blowout anyway because the worry had migrated from AI demand to AI financing. This morning the same name is being celebrated at a trillion dollars. Both things can be true. The demand is real and the pricing power in memory is extraordinary, and the market still has every right to question how the broader build-out gets paid for.

Our take is that today's chip rebound is a sentiment trade riding the Iran de-escalation and the deal flow, not a sign that the financing question has been answered. The names with genuine pricing power and self-funding cash flow, with Micron the obvious example, deserve to lead a recovery like this one. The parts of the complex that depend on continuous outside capital are the ones to watch on a day when the tape is generous, because generosity is exactly when those names rally hardest and tell you the least. We would use strength in the funding-dependent cohort to reduce, and treat the cash-generative memory and compute leaders as the higher-quality way to stay long the theme.

## Tesla: The Month's Worst Performer Has Its Own Problems

Worth flagging on a green morning is Tesla, which is closing out its worst month in over a year, down roughly 15 percent in June and weighed by a federal investigation into a fatal crash on top of the broader tech pullback. Jefferies cut its target to 375 dollars and floated the idea that Tesla increasingly trades as a SpaceX proxy as merger chatter builds, which would turn the stock into a tracker whose holders have to weigh dilution against optionality. Our read is that Tesla has decoupled from the AI-hardware bounce because its problems are idiosyncratic, regulatory, and structural rather than macro, and a relief rally in chips does not fix any of them. It is a reminder that not every megacap moves together when the index gaps higher.

## Iran: The Ceasefire That Broke and Was Patched in 48 Hours

The geopolitical story is the one that could still hijack everything, so it deserves a clear-eyed read. Over the weekend the fragile framework that had held since mid-June genuinely failed. The US struck five Iranian coastal sites, and Iran retaliated directly against the US Ali Al Salem Air Base in Kuwait and the Fifth Fleet headquarters in Bahrain, with drone barrages reported against Kuwait and the UAE. This was not a proxy skirmish. It was direct fire on American installations in the Gulf. Then, late Sunday, both sides blinked. A US official said the two would stand down for now, that vessels can move freely through the Strait of Hormuz, and that technical talks on the memorandum of understanding will continue, with a meeting set for Doha on Tuesday. Notably, the venue shifted from Switzerland and the agenda shifted from the nuclear file to the strait itself, which tells you what actually drove the weekend.

The reason markets can rally through this is that the one variable that moves the real economy, the strait, stayed open and is open now. But the signals are genuinely mixed and that is the risk. A senior US official insists nothing has been canceled and talks are on track, while Iran's deputy foreign minister Kazem Gharibabadi says technical talks are not yet scheduled and will happen only when conditions are met. Our read is that the market is right to price the de-escalation as the base case and wrong to treat it as settled. We just watched this ceasefire break in a single weekend. Oil is up about 1 percent this morning precisely because traders are no longer willing to fully discount the tail. The cheapest hedge in the market remains the one nobody wants to pay for when futures are green, and a second breakdown before or during the Doha talks is the headline that would put a fast bid back under crude and gold.

## Oil: Firmer on the Weekend Strikes, Capped by the Stand-Down

Crude is the cleanest read on how seriously to take the weekend. WTI is up roughly 1 percent toward 69.90 and Brent is near 73.10, firmer because the strikes were real, but nowhere near the panic levels a true Hormuz closure would produce, because the strait stayed open and both sides stood down. The structural bear case in oil is intact underneath the geopolitical noise. OPEC keeps adding barrels, Saudi Arabia is loading tankers at Ras Tanura in a visible output ramp, Iraq is pushing for a higher quota and even hinting it could exit the cartel, and Iranian crude continues to find a path to market. Oil gave back essentially its entire spring war premium over the past two weeks, and this morning's bounce is a modest rebuild of risk premium rather than a trend change. For portfolios the implication is unchanged. Cheap energy is still doing the Fed's disinflation work, and a small bid back into crude on a genuine weekend escalation does not undo that, it just reminds you the premium can come back quickly if Doha fails.

## The Fed and the Week's Real Catalyst: Thursday's Jobs Report

The macro backdrop has not softened and that is the ceiling on today's enthusiasm. Core PCE printed 3.4 percent in May, the hottest in well over a year, and Minneapolis Fed President Kashkari became the first official to openly pencil in a 2026 rate hike, citing broad inflation rather than the war. Into that, the calendar front-loads the one number that matters. Because July 4 falls on Saturday and the market closes early Friday, the June jobs report is pulled forward to Thursday, July 2, with consensus looking for a clear slowdown in hiring toward the low six figures. That report now sits at the center of the rate debate. A soft print revives the cut narrative and validates this bounce. A hot print hands the hawks their evidence and the long-duration tech names that are leading today give it all back. Treasuries are quiet ahead of it, with the ten-year near 4.38 percent and the two-year around 4.13 percent, the curve's way of saying cheaper oil is offsetting hawkish rhetoric for now. ISM Manufacturing on Wednesday and ADP are the warm-up acts. The week is light on earnings, with Nike on Tuesday night the main name, so the macro print is unusually dominant.

## Asia and Europe: A Steadier Overnight

The overnight tape abroad was constructive and far calmer than last week. Japan's Nikkei 225 clawed back early losses to finish a touch higher at 69,468.11, up about 0.2 percent, while South Korea's Kospi slipped a marginal 0.2 percent to 8,394.65, narrowing a sharper drop after Seoul flagged an investment package of more than 500 billion dollars. Greater China outperformed, with Hong Kong's Hang Seng jumping about 1.8 percent to 23,088.51 and the Shanghai Composite adding roughly 1.2 percent to 4,073.90, the region's tech and platform names leading as the US-Iran de-escalation steadied sentiment. Europe opened firmer in sympathy, with the Stoxx 600 up around half a percent and the DAX adding about 0.7 percent toward 24,850 as the chip and luxury names that led last week's declines bounced. The pattern is the mirror image of the prior week. Where Asia led the selling, it is now leading a tentative repair, and Europe is following at a fraction of the magnitude. With New York reopening into this, the early read is a synchronized relief bounce that still has to survive Thursday.

## Geopolitical Tracker

**US-Iran:** The ceasefire broke over the weekend and was patched within 48 hours. US strikes on five Iranian coastal sites drew direct Iranian fire on US bases in Kuwait and Bahrain and drone attacks on the UAE, before both sides agreed late Sunday to stand down, keep Hormuz open, and meet in Doha on Tuesday. The agenda has narrowed from the nuclear file to the strait, signals on whether talks are actually scheduled conflict between Washington and Tehran, and this remains the single largest swing factor for oil and risk appetite.

**Ukraine-Russia:** Structurally unresolved and once again overshadowed by the Gulf. Combat continues along the eastern axes and a pending US security-assistance package is the live item for fresh headline risk.

**Taiwan Strait and South China Sea:** PRC coast guard pressure near the Pratas Islands continues at a low simmer with no major escalation. The pattern of incremental activity persists but stays contained.
