Every future, yield, currency and commodity level in this note carries that stamp and can move before the bell. US bond markets are closed for Columbus Day while equities trade, so liquidity is thin and there is no major American data release today. Tokyo was shut for Sports Day. The big banks open earnings season tomorrow before the bell and September CPI lands Wednesday.

## Top Call: The Supplier Financing the Buildout Says Capital Is the Constraint

Hock Tan spoke in Singapore this morning at the Asia Future Forward conference and said that investors making outsized bets on artificial intelligence are, in his words, almost like they are in a casino gambling. He then said something more useful. Asked where the bottleneck sits, he answered that it keeps moving between wafers, memory, power and land, and that capital may now be the biggest constraint of all. He cited Firmus, the Nvidia backed data centre operator that pulled a $5.5bn Australian listing last week.

Consider who is saying it. Broadcom is arranging more than $110bn of the roughly $150bn of artificial intelligence financing this desk has been tracking since Wednesday: the $60bn package behind Anthropic's chip leases, whose senior tranche Broadcom is partially guaranteeing, and the $50bn plus Nexus programme it is building with OpenAI. The vendor that is also the lender has told the market that the thing it is short of is not silicon. That is the single most load bearing sentence anyone has produced on this subject in two weeks, and it came from the one party with no incentive to produce it.

Intel's Lip-Bu Tan was on the same panel and supplied the sector read. He expects hardware shortages to run two to three more years, and he named memory as the area most likely to lose momentum first.

Korea priced both statements inside the session. The Kospi fell 3.65% to 6,384.25, Samsung Electronics 4.39% to 250,500 won and SK Hynix 4.52% to 1.605m won. Nothing changed in either order book. What changed is that the two executives closest to the demand curve told an audience in Singapore that the constraint is money and that memory is where the music stops first.

Set against that, American futures are quiet and slightly higher, with the S&P contract above the index's October 6 record close, and Goldman Sachs raised global equities to overweight on a three month view this morning on a Q3 profit expectation near 25% growth. The same allocation note moved credit to underweight. That pairing is the whole argument in one page: buy the equity of the buildout, do not own the paper that funds it. It is this desk's thesis arriving through a strategist rather than a spread, and we will take it with the caveat we have been repeating, which is that an opinion is not a print.

We stay with capacity over claim. Today adds the qualifier we were missing, which is that owning the constraint does not protect you when the market decides the constraint has been fully paid for.

## Asia: The Bifurcation Test Resolves, and the Split Is Not Geographic

We pre-committed on Friday that Monday's Seoul open was the test. If Seoul followed Hong Kong's internet rebound rather than the mainland's hardware selloff, our geographic read was wrong.

Seoul followed the hardware chain, hard. The Kospi broke below 6,400 to close at 6,384.25. Taipei fell on TSMC. The structural detail underneath is worse than the index: Korean turnover is down roughly 70% from its late May peak, domestic retail participation is shrinking and foreign investors have accelerated outflows. The finance ministry said it will take stabilisation measures when necessary, citing elevated global bond yields, and the Bank of Korea governor apologised for the central bank's inadequate warnings on single stock leveraged exchange traded funds, calling its own June Financial Stability Report assessment inappropriate. A market where the central bank is apologising for retail leverage guidance while turnover collapses is not simply selling semiconductors. It is deleveraging.

Hong Kong closed up 0.25% at 24,271.20 on HK$162.6bn of turnover, but the Hang Seng Tech index fell 0.67% to 4,169. The mainland was heavier under the surface. The Shanghai Composite fell 0.43% to 3,797, the CSI 300 0.68% to 4,287.98, and the Shenzhen Component, which carries the hardware supply chain, dropped 2.03% to 12,384. Australia was the outlier, up 0.8% to 8,783 on financials and miners with BHP up 2.5% and Westpac 2.7%, though Telstra fell about 2.5% and TPG 4.9% as the SpaceX spectrum read across reached a third continent.

So the test passes on its stated terms and we are going to state what it actually produced. The division that showed up today is not American capacity against Chinese supply chain. Hong Kong rose while its own technology index fell. Australia rose on banks and iron ore while its telecoms fell. Korea fell on two chip names. The line runs through semiconductors, not through longitude, and we framed it as geography for three sessions. That is a real correction to the shape of the call even though the directional test resolved our way.

## The 20% Number: The Buildout Has Started Eating Its Own Downstream

IDC published preliminary third quarter personal computer data on October 8 and it is the most important number of the past week. Global shipments fell 20.1% year on year to 62.7m units from 78.5m, and 9.1% from the second quarter, which inverts the normal seasonal pattern in which the third quarter rises. Omdia, counting differently, has the decline at 21.2% to 58.1m, with desktops down 23.5% and laptops 20.6%.

The cause is not demand and it is not the consumer. Omdia estimates that dynamic random access memory and solid state drives now account for close to 40% of a personal computer's component cost, against roughly 15% before the shortage. Omdia projects a further 24% year on year decline in the fourth quarter and another 7% across 2027.

Put this beside last week's Apple news, where suppliers were told to cut October iPhone 18 Pro component orders by at least 15% after a $100 price increase traced directly to artificial intelligence driven memory costs. Two of the three largest consumer hardware categories have now repriced and lost volume on an input cost created by data centre demand.

The analytical point is about mix rather than morality. The memory bull case has rested on servers rising from roughly 30% of demand to 60% or 70%. Part of that shift is now arithmetic rather than server strength, because the denominator is shrinking. If personal computer units fall a further 24% in the fourth quarter, server share rises again without a single additional rack being installed. That makes the share statistic a weaker piece of evidence than it looked a month ago, and it is precisely what Intel's chief executive was pointing at when he said memory loses momentum first. IDC also notes that buyers stockpiled early in the year ahead of price rises, so some of this quarter is payback. That makes the fourth quarter the cleaner read, and Omdia already expects it to be worse.

## Energy and Geopolitics: Airports Are Now the Target Class

Brent is $106.80 and up 2.0%, WTI $93.79 and up 2.1%, the highest since September 29.

The path there matters. During the Asian session Brent traded down 1.2% near $103.46 on President Trump's announcement of an energy ceasefire between Russia and Ukraine. Through the European morning it sat near $104.17. It is now $106.80. A round trip of roughly three dollars in one session, driven entirely by two political headlines pulling opposite ways.

The bid is Saudi. A Houthi missile struck King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh on Saturday, killing 12 and wounding 309, the deadliest attack on the kingdom in more than a decade. One further person died in a second strike on Sunday. It was the third attack on that airport within a week, after Thursday's killed three including a pilot. This morning airports at Jizan and Najran were hit, wounding three. The dead at Riyadh included an American citizen alongside Saudi, Bangladeshi, Jordanian, Palestinian, Syrian, Sudanese and Egyptian nationals. The Houthis have claimed the attacks and warned airlines that Saudi airports are potential targets. Riyadh has promised to respond decisively and President Trump said he is weighing joining Saudi strikes on the group.

The offer is Russian. Trump posted on Sunday that, effective immediately, there is an energy ceasefire in the war between Russia and Ukraine. Moscow has not confirmed it. Kyiv reported Russian strikes on a Kharkiv thermal power plant afterwards. Zelensky said Ukraine will agree if Russia does, which is a conditional acceptance of something that has not been offered. This is the second such announcement in roughly a month and the first was never confirmed either.

Two conclusions. First, our standing rule that supply side policy responses in this conflict are worth about one session needs shortening. This one was worth a few hours. Second, and more durable, the target set has changed. For six weeks the risk has been priced through tankers, refineries and the strait, all of which are insurable, reroutable and in the end substitutable. Civil aviation is none of those things. An explicit warning to airlines over Saudi airports raises war risk premiums on an asset class that has no maritime equivalent, and it introduces the one variable most likely to move Washington before November 3, which is an American civilian casualty. A ceasefire headline can be withdrawn. An airport that insurers have repriced cannot be.

## Rates: A Closed Market Ahead of the Week's Only Number

The Treasury market is shut today. Friday left the ten year at 5.24%, the thirty year near 5.60% and the two year 4.80%, with the policy range at 3.75% to 4.00%.

September CPI arrives Wednesday at 8:30. Consensus is 3.6% to 3.7% year on year against 3.4% in August, with the Cleveland Fed nowcast at 3.60% and core expected at 0.2% month on month. Most of the expected jump is energy, and specifically record September pump prices, which is the same force that drove the University of Michigan index to 46.3 with one year inflation expectations at 4.7% and the five to ten year gauge at 3.5%.

October is priced near 19% to 20% and December between 70% and 81%. A headline at 3.7% driven by petrol with core at 0.2% changes nothing, because that is the distribution the curve already holds. The asymmetry is core at 0.3% or above, which would put October back in play. The awkward feature of this particular week is that the bond market cannot express a view today, so whatever positioning would normally happen ahead of a pivotal print has to be compressed into Tuesday.

## Equities: Seven Points From a Record, With the Wrong Leadership

Friday closed strong. The S&P 500 rose 0.59% to 7,811.54, which left it seven points below the October 6 record close of 7,818.93 and well under the 7,844.52 intraday high. The Nasdaq Composite added 0.64% to 27,366.17, the Dow 0.83% to 51,654.95 and the Russell 0.46% to 2,806.98.

This morning the S&P contract is 7,866.00 and up 0.08%, Dow futures 51,943 and up 0.03%, Nasdaq 100 futures 31,084.50 and down 0.08% and Russell futures 2,819.20 and down 0.10%. The VIX is 15.49 and up 4.4%.

That last pair is the honest signal on the screen. Flat futures with volatility bid 4.4% is a market that is not selling but is paying up for protection into a week containing four bank prints, a CPI release and an open question about whether the United States joins a Saudi air campaign. The bull market turned four years old today, and the index is up about 13.5% year to date, though stripping out artificial intelligence related names leaves the American market slightly behind Europe for the year. That is worth holding onto when reading any index level this week: the record is an artificial intelligence record, and the complex that produces it fell 4% in Seoul overnight.

## Europe: Banks Lead Into the Earnings Week

The Stoxx 600 is 635.09 and up 0.56%, the DAX 25,207 and up 0.48%, the FTSE 100 10,613.47 and up about 0.6%, with the CAC 40 and FTSE MIB also higher. Banks led the advance as crude eased during the European morning on the ceasefire headline and eurozone yields fell.

The euro is 1.1204, sterling 1.3243 and dollar yen 158.42. LVMH, ASML and EQT report this week, with ASML's third quarter on Wednesday guided to €11bn to €12bn of revenue and a 55% to 57% gross margin against €9.33bn in the second quarter.

European banks leading on the same day Korean memory fell four and a half percent is the cleanest available statement of what is actually rotating. The money leaving the semiconductor complex is not going to cash. It is going into balance sheets that benefit from a curve nobody expects to come down.

## Corporate: Two Opposite Calls on the Same Question

**Salesforce** is up about 4% after Bank of America double upgraded it, arguing that artificial intelligence should deepen customer dependence on the platform rather than disrupt it. **Adobe** is down about 4% after Wells Fargo double downgraded it to Underweight and cut its target to $225 from $270. Two firms published opposite conclusions on the same morning about the same question, which is whether generative models erode application software. We read the pair as progress rather than noise. For a year the sell side treated software disruption as a sector call. It is now being resolved name by name on the specific defensibility of each workflow, which is the only level at which the question has an answer.

**IREN** is up about 5% on a report in The Information that it has signed a multiyear artificial intelligence cloud agreement with Anthropic. Neither company has announced it. Anthropic's confidential listing prospectus lists at least $518bn of planned infrastructure spending across six partners. A former bitcoin miner is now a marginal supplier of frontier compute, and the terms reach the market through a leak rather than a filing. That is the disclosure problem we have been writing about since Thursday, appearing again on the supply side rather than the revenue side.

**Bristol-Myers Squibb** is up about 5.7% on positive topline results from two pivotal Phase 3 trials. **Biogen** is up about 2% on a JPMorgan upgrade citing 2027 readouts. **Humana** was raised to Outperform at Raymond James with a $520 target after confirming 95% of its Medicare Advantage members will sit in four star or better plans for 2027, following an 11.56% gain on Friday to $431.87. The managed care complex has repriced violently on a quality rating that is formulaic and knowable, which says more about how thin positioning had become than about the business.

**CCC Intelligent Solutions** is up 9% to 10% on reports that GTCR and Elliott are in advanced talks to acquire it. **IonQ** is down 2% to 3% on agreeing to buy the cybersecurity firm Elisity for up to $1.3bn in stock. **Nvidia** is in early talks to acquire, acqui-hire or further invest in Reflection AI according to the Financial Times, having already put $800m into a company that went from an $8bn valuation last October to $25bn in March. The acqui-hire structure is reportedly preferred because it avoids a lengthy regulatory review, which is now a standard feature of this cycle rather than an exception. **Stellantis** is higher on an understanding between Brussels and Beijing that could sharply reduce Chinese hybrid exports to the bloc, and **Netflix** is weighing a workforce reduction of roughly 5%, close to 850 roles, ahead of its October 20 results.

## Earnings: The Banks Tomorrow, and What Samsung Already Told Us

Nothing in our coverage reported overnight. The four banks tomorrow are the real event.

| Company | LSEG consensus EPS | What actually decides the stock |
|---|---|---|
| JPMorgan | $5.94 | 2027 funding cost guidance, card delinquencies |
| Goldman Sachs | $12.44 | Trading on a record commodity tape, IB pipeline |
| Citigroup | $2.41 | Credit costs in cards, expense trajectory |
| Wells Fargo | $1.85 | Net interest income guide, down 13% in a month |

**Our take**: the quarter is not the point. The S&P banks index has fallen about 7.5% in a month and JPMorgan sits roughly 10% below its August peak near $368 after closing Friday at $332.99, so a beat is partly in the price already. Options are pricing moves of 3.65% to 5%. Trading should be strong given volatile rates and a record commodities tape, and that is the fastest route to a positive reaction. The number that can spoil it is provisions. Consumer sentiment is at 46.3, pump prices are above $4 in parts of the country and retail diesel printed through its 2022 record last week, which is exactly the household that shows up in card delinquencies one or two quarters later. The structural bull case is the investment banking rebound, and the uncomfortable fact is that the equity issuance window just closed on Firmus while the credit being underwritten behind it is the paper Goldman's own strategists moved to underweight this morning.

**Samsung Electronics** deserves a second look because of what happened to it today. Preliminary third quarter operating profit of 107.4tn won, up roughly 783% and the first quarter above 100tn won in South Korean history, was published on October 8. Revenue of about 195tn came in below forecasts clustered between 199tn and 207tn. The shares have now fallen on both sessions since, 2.4% then 4.4%, for a cumulative decline near 7% after the best operating quarter the company has ever printed.

**Our take**: the market is pricing the second derivative of memory pricing, not the level. An operating profit that multiplied eightfold tells you where the cycle has been. The revenue shortfall against sell side forecasts tells you that the volume assumptions behind those forecasts were too high, and this morning's personal computer data explains why. When the best print in a company's history produces two down sessions, the argument has stopped being about whether earnings are good and started being about how many quarters of them are left. Intel's chief executive gave that argument a name today.

## Accountability

**The bifurcation test resolved our way and the frame was still wrong.** We said on Friday that if Seoul followed Hong Kong's internet rebound rather than the mainland hardware selloff, our geographic read was wrong. Seoul fell 3.65% and followed the hardware chain, so the stated test passes. We are not going to take more than that gives us. Our underlying claim was that physical capacity is the asset and the financial representation of demand is the liability. Seoul sold the physical capacity. Samsung and SK Hynix are the two most capacity constrained assets in the complex and they fell 4.4% and 4.5% with nothing changed in either order book. Meanwhile Hong Kong rose while its own technology index fell and Australia rose while its telecoms fell. The division that exists is semiconductors against everything else. We described it as geography for three sessions and that was the wrong axis.

**We confessed to missing the personal computer demand destruction while the number was already public.** On Friday we admitted that we had been modelling the memory upcycle as one sided after Apple's order cut, and called that a genuine miss. IDC published its preliminary third quarter data on October 8, the day before we wrote that, showing shipments down 20.1%. We did not have it. The confession was correct and the research behind it was late, which is the worse of the two failures, because the evidence was sitting in public when we apologised for not having the thesis.

**Our funding thesis got its strongest endorsement from the wrong kind of source and we will name the limit.** Hock Tan saying capital may be the biggest constraint, while his own company arranges the debt, is the most authoritative version of this argument anyone has made. It is still an executive's opinion. High yield spreads were 305 basis points on Wednesday, the bond market was closed today, and we still cannot mark the instrument we said we would be marked on. Goldman moving credit to underweight is positioning, not a print either. We wrote on Saturday that we would be wrong about the mechanism if CPI and the October FOMC pass without spreads widening materially from 305. That test is intact, unresolved, and we are not claiming it early.

**The one session rule for policy headlines is now a few hours.** We have written repeatedly that supply side political responses in this conflict are worth roughly one session. Trump's energy ceasefire took Brent down more than 1% in Asia and was fully reversed before the American open by a Saudi escalation that had already happened on Saturday. Calling it a one session rule overstates the half life, and in this case the market reversed a headline using information it already had.

**We had no view on civil aviation and that is where the escalation went.** We have tracked tankers, refineries, insurance and the strait for six weeks. Three Saudi airports have now been struck within a week, twelve people are dead at Riyadh including an American, and the Houthis have told airlines directly that Saudi airports are targets. We did not write a word about aviation risk and it is now the channel carrying the variable most likely to change American policy before November 3. We will carry it from here.
