Saturday, August 29, 2026, written after Friday's close, so every level below is a settlement rather than a moving mark and nothing changes until Monday's Asian open. Jackson Hole's final panels run today and rarely matter. Yesterday's keynote mattered a great deal, and the interesting part is not that a hawkish Fed chair sold gold. It is which parts of the market paid and which did not.

## Top Call: The Chair Defended the Currency, and the Currency Hedge Paid for It

Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote as chair moved September odds for a twenty-five basis point hike from roughly 35% to 57.5% in a single morning. Gold fell 3.13% to $4,456, its worst session of the year, on the last Friday of what is still its best month since 1999. Silver fell 4.24% to $66.21. The two-year rose 11.8 basis points to 4.348%. The S&P 500 fell a quarter of one percent and still finished the week higher.

That split is the story. For three weeks this note has tracked a debasement trade built on one premise, that the fiscal authority was setting the price of long money and no one at the monetary authority was going to argue. Warsh argued. The assets that were owned as protection against an absent central bank were the assets that got marked down, and the equity market, which is not a currency hedge, barely flinched.

## The Number That Did It

Warsh gave no forward guidance and no reaction function, which he had promised in advance and delivered on. What he gave instead was arithmetic, and it is more useful than guidance would have been.

| Warsh's inflation case | Reading | Why it binds |
|---|---|---|
| **PCE, twelve-month** | 3.7% | a three-year high, already known |
| **PCE, six-month annualised** | **4.1%** | the trend is running above the level |
| **Components rising above 3%** | 54% of the basket | against 32% pre-pandemic |
| **Jobless rate** | 4.1% | no labour market excuse for patience |
| **Business capex, four-quarter** | roughly 9% | demand is not the problem |

The six-month figure is the one to carry forward. Headline PCE at 3.7% printed on Wednesday and the market treated it as an old number attached to a fading impulse. Warsh pointed out that the shorter window is running hotter than the longer one, which is the definition of an inflation process that has stopped decelerating. His sentence on the summer data was blunt: those prints "do not tell me that underlying trends have meaningfully improved." Set beside a breadth statistic showing more than half the consumption basket rising faster than three percent, against a third of it before the pandemic, and the case for waiting gets thin.

The formal commitment was equally plain. "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have work to do." That is not guidance. It is a condition, and the condition is not currently met.

## The Curve Is Where the Real Information Sits

Almost every account of Friday led with gold. The more valuable move was in Treasuries, and it only shows up when the week is taken as a whole rather than the day.

| | Fri Aug 21 | Fri Aug 28 | Week |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2Y** | 4.12% | 4.348% | **+23bp** |
| **10Y** | 4.73% | 4.72% | -1bp |
| **30Y** | 5.27% | 5.21% | **-6bp** |
| 2s30s | 115bp | 86bp | flattened 29bp |

The market repriced the front end violently and the long end went the other way. That is a bear flattening, and it is the pattern you get when a central bank is judged more likely to control inflation rather than less. Term premium at the back of the curve is compensation for the risk that the policy framework tolerates a higher average price level over decades. A chair who says the fight is not finished reduces exactly that risk, and he does it by promising pain at the front.

It is worth naming what this means for the intervention debate of the past fortnight. The Treasury doubled its long-bond buybacks on August 19 and the thirty-year gave back every basis point of relief within three sessions, which this note called the most analytically useful event of that week. On Friday a speech with no operation attached to it did what $4 billion per operation could not. The long end does not want a bigger official bid. It wants a credible inflation target, and it will pay for one.

## Gold, Silver and the Composition of the Unwind

Gold's 3.13% fall to $4,456 needs the month around it. The metal is still up roughly 9.5% in August with one session left on Monday, and that remains its strongest month since 1999. December futures settled 3.35% lower at $4,529.90. Bitcoin traded above $81,000 before the speech and closed near $77,589, down 2.22%, finishing roughly where it started the week after the previous week's 22% surge.

The gold to silver ratio is the tell. It compressed to 65.7 during the debasement rally, which this note read at the time as evidence of a monetary bid rather than a fear bid, because silver only keeps pace when flow is real. On Friday silver fell harder than gold and the ratio widened back to about 67.3. The same indicator that confirmed the trade going up confirmed it coming down, and the symmetry is the point: this was a position unwind in the currency-hedge complex, not a flight to safety and not a growth scare. A growth scare does not leave the S&P down a quarter percent and the VIX at 14.35.

## The Earnings That Mattered

Three prints defined the week and all three sit inside the AI capital cycle. The market's treatment of them was consistent in a way that is easy to miss.

| | Result | Reaction | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nvidia** (Wed) | $96.22B vs $92.17B, EPS $2.22 vs $2.09, guides $108B, FY28 growth roughly 70% | **+8.7% Thu**, gave back about 3% Fri | demand settled, margin to 71-72% by January on memory |
| **Marvell** (Thu) | record $2.739B, +37%, data centre $2.17B +46%, raised FY27 and FY28 | **-7%** | gross margin guided lower on custom silicon mix |
| **Salesforce** (Wed) | $11.345B, EPS $5.90, FY27 raised to $46.1-46.4B, cRPO +14% | **+22.6% Thu** | Agentforce annualised above $1.5B, Claude at the centre of Slack |

**Our take**: the tape paid for proof and charged for mix. Nvidia and Marvell both beat, both raised, and both were marked down on gross margin rather than on demand, which is now the third consecutive week in which the argument about AI has been about who keeps the money rather than whether the money gets spent. The two cases are not the same and the distinction still holds a week later. Nvidia's compression is cyclical, driven by memory costs that reverse when HBM supply catches up. Marvell's is structural, because custom silicon is priced closer to cost plus a fee, so its margin falls precisely when it wins. Salesforce is the counterweight and the most underrated print of the week: a software company that showed AI revenue actually landing in the reported line rather than in a pipeline number got a twenty-two percent day, which tells you the premium is now on evidence rather than on narrative.

Warsh, unexpectedly, supplied the macro version of the same evidence. He put annualised token sales at the two leading labs above $100 billion, up more than 500% year on year, called AI "a new variable, potentially a new factor of production," and said he is watching the second derivative of capital spending, meaning whether the growth rate is still accelerating. A sitting Fed chair treating AI capex as a macro variable rather than a sector story is a meaningful change in how this cycle will be discussed, and it cuts both ways: if AI raises potential output it is disinflationary over time, and if it mostly raises demand for power, chips and construction labour first, it is inflationary now. He declined to say which, which is honest.

## Sector Spotlights

**Semiconductors and memory.** The supply story hardened again this week. Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron have effectively sold HBM capacity through 2027, and reports have Nvidia testing cut-down Rubin Ultra configurations with as little as 192GB of memory, which is a rationing signal rather than a design choice. That is the direct source of Nvidia's own margin guidance. The competitive development that keeps shifting the mix is OpenAI and Broadcom's Jalapeño inference part, built on TSMC three-nanometre with six HBM4 stacks for 216GiB at 15.4TB/s, benchmarked by OpenAI at up to 1.9 times the throughput per kilowatt of Nvidia's flagship. Where power rather than silicon is the binding constraint, every large buyer has a reason to route more inference through a custom program, and custom is the lower-margin end for the suppliers.

**Financials and Europe's fiscal risk.** French banks were the week's clearest single-country stress, with Societe Generale, Credit Agricole and BNP Paribas falling between 3.3% and 4.3% on Thursday as the CAC 40 slid roughly 1.7% to a one-month low. The mechanism is the sovereign, not the loan book. The France-Germany ten-year spread has widened to about 80 basis points, its widest in nearly nine months, and French yields now trade above Italy's despite Rome carrying more debt. Prime Minister Sebastien Lecornu has warned the deficit could reach 6.5% of GDP without a new budget, and two prime ministers have already fallen over budget disputes since 2024. This is the same duration repricing that has driven US and Japanese long ends all month, arriving in the one large European economy with an election calendar attached.

**Energy.** Brent settled at $89.45 and WTI at $83.31, down 5.3% and 4.3% on the week, ending a two-week advance. The driver is the reclassification of the Iran problem from a physical supply threat to a sanctions and economics confrontation. Goldman Sachs put Persian Gulf crude exports back at roughly two-thirds of pre-war levels, or 15 to 16 million barrels a day, against a March trough of 5 to 6 million. Falling crude into a hawkish Fed is an awkward combination for the inflation debate, because it removes the one component most likely to have pulled headline PCE back toward target on its own.

**Healthcare.** A quietly productive week for approvals. The FDA cleared the 2026-2027 Comirnaty formulation from Pfizer and BioNTech targeting the XFG variant, approved Bixlenvo as the smallest once-daily single-tablet HIV regimen for virologically suppressed adults, and approved Priovant's Lisraya in dermatomyositis. Cytokinetics presented full Phase III aficamten data in non-obstructive HCM at ESC in Munich with simultaneous publication in the New England Journal of Medicine.

## Asia and Europe

Asia finished Friday mostly higher on Nvidia's coattails, before Warsh spoke. The Nikkei rose 0.41% to 66,405.56, the Hang Seng added 0.07% to 25,584.79, the Shanghai Composite slipped 0.11% to 3,952.18 and the CSI 300 fell 0.46% to 4,609.18. Korea was the outlier and the more informative one, with the Kospi down 1.79% to 6,788.88 on a second consecutive session of profit-taking in AI chips. Korea has been the purest expression of the memory cycle all year, and two down days there against a euphoric US semiconductor session is worth watching rather than dismissing.

Europe recovered from a poor Thursday. The Stoxx 600 rose about 0.5% to roughly 655 after Thursday's 0.7% drop to 651.85, the DAX gained 0.6% to 26,523.10 and the FTSE 100 added 0.2% to 10,815.07. The DAX sits close to its record while French assets carry a discount that is entirely political, which is an unusually wide dispersion inside a single currency union and the thing to watch in Europe through the autumn budget season.

Tokyo core CPI accelerated for a third consecutive month to 1.8% from 1.7% on Friday, even with government energy subsidies pushing the other way. Together with a US chair signalling more work to do, two of the three largest bond markets in the world are now repricing toward higher terminal inflation at the same time.

## Geopolitical Risks

The Strait of Hormuz continues to normalise slowly and unevenly. Transits rose more than 30% week on week to 114 between August 17 and 24, led by tankers and gas carriers, which is a genuine improvement and still far below the pre-war baseline of roughly 73 transits per day. Iran and Oman have agreed a temporary maritime corridor, but Tehran has been explicit that full reopening waits on US compliance with an interim deal signed in June that has since lapsed, and the proposed arrangement would route traffic through a fee-collecting corporation rather than restoring freedom of navigation. That is a structural change to a chokepoint, not a return to the prior state, and it is the reason the crude curve keeps a premium that neither collapses nor extends.

Ukraine has continued striking Russian refining capacity through August, including Ryazan, Yaroslavl, Kuibyshev and Afipsky. This remains a diesel crack story rather than a Brent story, because destroying refining capacity does not destroy crude supply, and it explains why the oil complex can fall more than five percent in a week that contained more than a dozen strikes on Russian energy infrastructure. Ukraine's interceptor stocks are the more consequential problem heading into a winter in which Russia has historically targeted the grid.

Friday's payrolls is the event that decides whether 57% becomes 80% or falls back under a coin flip. The asymmetry is unusual: a soft print no longer buys much dovish relief, because Warsh has just told the market that the labour side is not his binding constraint at a 4.1% jobless rate, while a firm print lands on a committee that has already dissented three ways toward a hike. Dell on Tuesday and Broadcom on Wednesday extend the week's margin question from chips into systems and into the largest custom silicon franchise in the market.

## Accountability

Two corrections to entries in this week's notes. Thursday's edition described gold's move into the keynote as the market pricing a chair who would validate the debasement trade, and that read was wrong in direction and in size; the single largest one-day fall in gold this year followed within thirty hours. Friday's edition also carried the PayPal buyout at "$53 billion-plus" from the original reported terms, while several accounts of the abandoned Advent and Stripe approach put it closer to $50 billion; the stock's 12% to 16% fall to roughly the $60.50 bid its own board had called inadequate is unaffected either way, and that remains the cleanest available read on what large leveraged financing costs right now.
