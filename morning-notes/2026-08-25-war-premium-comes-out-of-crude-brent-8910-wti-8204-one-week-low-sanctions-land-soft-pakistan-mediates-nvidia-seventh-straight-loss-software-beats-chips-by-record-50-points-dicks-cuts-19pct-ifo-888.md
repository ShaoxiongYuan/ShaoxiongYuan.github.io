Tuesday, August 25, 2026, written at 9:15 a.m. Eastern, fifteen minutes before the cash open, so every US level below is a pre-market mark and the 10 a.m. confidence print has not landed. Yesterday's two o'clock headline has now printed, and the most important thing about it is what it did not contain.

## Top Call: The War Premium Comes Out of Crude

Brent is $89.10, down 3.33%, and WTI is $82.04, down 3.49%, both at one-week lows. Scott Bessent's "Operation Economic Outcast" designated close to sixty entities, individuals and vessels and opened five new sectoral determinations covering digital assets, technology, gold, aviation and shipping, but it named no target countries, published no compliance deadlines and deferred the hardest measure. On the same morning Pakistan's Field Marshal Asim Munir left Tehran after a one-day visit carrying proposals for sanctions relief under an existing memorandum, having met President Pezeshkian and Foreign Minister Araghchi.

Our read: the market has decided that economic pressure is a substitute for military escalation rather than a prelude to it, and it is unwinding six months of war premium on that judgment. That is a defensible trade and an incomplete one. Bessent explicitly declined to exempt China, which buys more than eighty percent of Iran's seaborne crude, and said only that Washington does "not have infinite patience." A regime with no deadline is not a soft regime; it is an undated one. What the tape is really pricing is timing, not severity, and the barrels most at risk sit behind a strait where shipments are already near a standstill.

The second-order effect is the one that matters for everything else on this page. Cheaper crude is what has been carrying the September hike bid all summer, and with Brent thirty dollars below its late-July high near $105, September hike odds have fallen to roughly thirty percent on the CME caliber against a pause near seventy. That is a meaningfully different number from the low forties carried in this book last week, and it lands two days before core PCE and three before Kevin Warsh's first Jackson Hole keynote.

## Operation Economic Outcast: What Landed, and What Was Held Back

The designations are real. Close to sixty corporations, individuals and vessels across multiple jurisdictions, including several Chinese nationals, tied to nuclear and missile procurement, cyber operations and oil smuggling. Five sectoral determinations give Treasury the standing authority to reach any foreign firm touching Iranian digital assets, technology, gold, aviation or shipping. That is a wider legal perimeter than anything the campaign has previously claimed.

What was withheld is equally deliberate. No countries were named. No entity-level deadlines were disclosed. The Washington Post characterisation, that the package is sweeping but delays the toughest blow, is the accurate one. Trump has said the countries still trading with Tehran will be given a specific timeline to sever ties or face unilateral penalties, and that timeline is precisely the piece that has not been published.

We think the market is half right and is being paid to be half right for now. Optionality retained by Treasury is optionality not yet priced by anyone, and a sanctions architecture that can be switched on against Chinese refiners in a single Federal Register notice is a live tail into the late-September US-China summit. The Canada file is the cautionary precedent: talks collapsed on August 21, fifty percent tariffs on roughly twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods took effect on the 22nd, Ottawa retaliates dollar for dollar on September 8, and the tape has still not moved for it. Crude, notably, was carved out of that package. Energy keeps getting the exemption, and each time it does the market extrapolates the exemption forward.

## The Widest Software-Over-Chips Gap Ever Recorded, Into Nvidia Tomorrow Night

Since June 22 the equal-weight software fund has gained about twenty-four percent while the equal-weight semiconductor fund has lost about twenty-four percent. That fifty-point spread is the widest software-over-chips move in the history of either vehicle, back to 2011. The breadth beneath it is not close: 37 of 45 software names are higher over that window, and 59 of 60 chip names are lower.

Nvidia sits at the centre of it. The stock fell for a seventh consecutive session on Monday, its longest losing run since September 2022, down roughly seven percent over the streak against 1.2% for the S&P, and it trades near $210 into a print on Wednesday after the close. Consensus is about $91.85 billion of revenue and $2.08 of earnings against a company guide of $91 billion plus or minus two percent. Monday's specific damage came from Micron, off 5.8% on fresh Netlist patent pressure, and from a Bloomberg report that Nvidia has told its largest customers that AI server prices are going up more than fifteen percent in many cases for early-next-year shipments, including Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell, because of memory costs.

That price increase is the analytical heart of the divergence, and it deserves to be stated plainly rather than treated as a margin footnote. The cost of a unit of compute at the rack level is rising while the price a lab can charge for a unit of AI output is falling. Software captures that gap and silicon absorbs it. Hyperscalers understood this before the tape did, which is why Meta, Amazon and Alphabet all closed higher on Monday between 0.9% and 1.7% while the semiconductor fund fell 2.7%, the widest chip-versus-cloud split in weeks. AMD is the one exception this morning, up 2.5% pre-market after Raymond James moved to Strong Buy with a $641 target from $565, arguing a 44% five-year compound growth rate in AI server CPUs toward roughly $201 billion by 2030.

The bar for Wednesday is therefore not the printed consensus. It is whether Nvidia can show that a fifteen percent price increase sticks without demand destruction, because that is the number that decides whether the memory squeeze is a cost problem or a pricing power demonstration. We note without enthusiasm that this is the same structural setup Samsung had on Monday, when a package delivered at the top of its reported range still took the stock down 8.7%: what is published and what is positioned are not the same bar.

## Rates: The Treasury Reaches for the Cash Pile

The ten-year is 4.66% and the thirty-year 5.19%, both down about four basis points, after CNBC reported that Treasury could tap its General Account, built up to roughly $950 billion, to fund the expanded long-end buyback programme. That is a departure from the prevailing expectation, which was that the doubled buybacks, raised from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, would be financed with new bill issuance in what Bessent has called a Treasury Twist. Officials did not rule out the bill route and did not say how much of the account would be used.

The mechanism matters more than the headline. Funding buybacks with bills swaps duration for front-end supply and leaves the debt stock unchanged; funding them from the cash balance retires long paper without issuing anything, which is a genuine reduction in duration outstanding and a genuine drawdown of the buffer that exists for exactly the kind of fiscal accident this is designed to prevent. Last week's version of this news bought about nine basis points and gave all of it back inside three sessions. This version has bought four so far. We would treat the fade rate as the useful signal: each successive intervention is purchasing less relief for a larger commitment, which is the definition of diminishing returns on a policy that has not yet been tested by an actual auction.

The Case-Shiller release at nine o'clock fits the same disinflationary picture. National home prices rose 1.9% year on year in June, down from 2.3%, with the twenty-city composite at 2.1% from 2.8% and the ten-city at 2.6% from 3.4%. New York led at 7.0% and Tampa fell 2.4%. Shelter is decelerating on the leading measure while the market argues about whether energy has stopped adding to the headline.

## The Read

Three observations into the open, and they are connected by one variable.

The first is that the energy shock has been the engine of the entire 2026 hike narrative, and this morning it is running in reverse. Brent at $89 against $105 in late July, with a sanctions regime that arrived without deadlines and a mediation channel that just reopened, removes the single largest upside risk to the inflation path two days before core PCE. If Warsh wants room to sound structural rather than hawkish on Friday, the oil tape has just handed it to him.

The second is that the AI complex has stopped trading as one asset. Fifty points of dispersion between software and silicon in nine weeks, with 59 of 60 chip names on the wrong side of it, is not rotation noise; it is the market separating who pays the compute bill from who collects on it. Nvidia's fifteen percent server price increase is the mechanism made explicit, and Wednesday night is where that gets adjudicated rather than debated.

The third is that Europe is quietly having the best quarter of any developed market and almost nobody is discussing it. Ifo at a one-year high, GDP revised up, the DAX through 26,300 and defence leading, all of it into cheaper energy rather than out of it. That combination has not existed in Germany since before the war began.

Written pre-market. The ten o'clock confidence print can change the household read, and both Wednesday morning and Wednesday night can change everything above it.
