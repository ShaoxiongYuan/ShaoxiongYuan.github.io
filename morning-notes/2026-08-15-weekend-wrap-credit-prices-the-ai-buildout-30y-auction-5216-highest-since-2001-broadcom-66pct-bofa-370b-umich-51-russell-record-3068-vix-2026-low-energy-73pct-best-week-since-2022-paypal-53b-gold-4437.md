Saturday, August 15, 2026, written after Friday's close, so every level below is a settlement rather than a moving mark, and the next thing that can change any of it is Monday's Asian open. The week produced a record close in the Russell 2000, a record in the S&P MidCap 400, the lowest VIX print of 2026, the fourth lightest volume of the year, and the most expensive thirty-year Treasury auction since 2001. Those facts sit awkwardly together, and reconciling them is the whole note.

## Top Call: Equity Priced the Buildout, Credit Started Pricing the Debt

Two things happened this week that the equity tape treated as unrelated and that we think are one event. On Thursday the Treasury sold $25 billion of thirty-year paper at 5.216%, the highest yield at that tenor since 2001, with a bid-to-cover of 2.39 and an awarded yield above the when-issued level, which means demand came in soft rather than merely adequate. A day earlier the ten-year auction drew the highest financing cost since 2007. On Friday Broadcom fell 6.6% after a Bank of America analyst downgraded its issuer and bond ratings on an estimate that the AI XPV financing platform it built with Apollo and Blackstone could carry roughly $370 billion of senior debt by mid-2029 at twenty gigawatts of scale, including about $150 billion of new issuance in 2027 alone.

Our take: the sovereign and the semiconductor are competing for the same pool of long-duration capital, and this was the first week the market charged both of them for it. Nvidia's announcement on August 10 that six Wall Street firms will mobilise more than $500 billion of third-party capital against GPU collateral is the mechanism that makes the two markets one market. Compute is being converted into a securitised asset class, which is genuinely clever financial engineering and also means the buildout now has a coupon. An equity investor gets to believe the capex thesis without a maturity date. A credit investor does not, and credit is who has to fund the next leg. We would treat the long end of the Treasury curve as the single most important input for AI infrastructure equities from here, which is not a sentence anyone was writing in January.

Thursday's session took the S&P above 7,800 intraday and to a record 7,798.99 close, its twenty-seventh record of the year. Friday gave a small piece back. What matters more than the index is the composition underneath it. Energy added 7.3% for its best week since October 2022. Six of eleven sectors finished higher. Excluding energy, the spread between the best and worst sector on the week was 1.2%, which is close to no dispersion at all. Small and mid caps set records on a day the megacap complex sold. That is a market broadening out rather than one narrowing into a handful of names, and it is the strongest argument for the bull case right now.

The argument against sits in the same table. A VIX at its lowest level of the year, on the fourth lightest volume of the year, describes a market where very few people are trading and nobody is paying for protection, three weeks after the thirty-year auction cleared at a twenty-five-year high. Reported positioning data shows demand for downside hedges quietly increasing even as institutions keep buying equity, which is what people do when they believe the trend and distrust the level.

## The Auction Almost Nobody Discussed

| 30Y Treasury Auction (Aug 13) | Result | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Size | $25B | scheduled |
| Awarded yield | 5.216% | highest since 2001 |
| Bid-to-cover | 2.39 | below 12-month average |
| Primary dealer take | 11.5% | dealers absorbed the gap |
| July comparison | 5.06% | 16bp worse in one month |

The tail is the detail that matters. The awarded yield came in above the when-issued level, meaning buyers demanded a concession rather than reaching for the paper, and primary dealers were left holding 11.5% of the issue. Ten-year yields have now sat above 4.6% for roughly a month, about forty basis points above the Congressional Budget Office's own projection path. The July auction cleared at 5.06% and the comparable print before the current administration took office was 4.91%.

Our take: this is a term premium story rather than a policy story, and the distinction changes what a dovish data run is worth. Four consecutive soft macro prints this month, negative payrolls, in-line CPI, flat PPI and a 0.6% drop in retail sales, took the front end down and the September hike probability into the low forties. The long end went the other way. When the two-year rallies on weak data and the thirty-year sells at auction, the market is telling you that its worry is not the next twenty-five basis points of policy, it is the arithmetic of funding a forty-trillion-dollar debt stock into a widening deficit. Equity multiples that depend on a discount rate are exposed to the second problem and get no help from the first.

## Michigan Confirms What Retail Sales Said

| Expectations | 55.4 | 50.6 | ends two-month recovery |

| 1yr inflation expectation | 4.2% | 4.3% | still far above 3.4% pre-war |
| 5-10yr expectation | 3.3% | 3.3% | anchored, third month |

Friday morning's release landed forty minutes after we published Thursday's note and it confirmed the direction of Thursday's retail sales print rather than contradicting it. Expected business conditions fell 11% over the short run and 17% over the long run. Only 8% of respondents expect their income to grow faster than inflation. The declines were concentrated among older, lower-income and non-college households, the exact cohort with no financial asset exposure to the rally that has taken the index to twenty-seven records this year.

Our take: sentiment at 51.0 with long-run inflation expectations anchored at 3.3% is a real income complaint, not an inflation-expectations problem, and that is the reading the Fed will take. It reinforces the case against a September hike and it does very little damage to the market, because the earnings driving this tape do not run through the American household. Yesterday we argued that this market is a supply-side capital cycle rather than a demand-led expansion, and Michigan is the second data point in two days supporting it. The uncomfortable version of that argument is that an economy where sentiment sits at 51 and equities sit at records has a distribution problem that eventually becomes a policy problem, and policy risk is not something equity multiples are currently paying for.

| **Nebius** | Revenue +454% | higher |

Applied Materials is the one worth dwelling on because it settled a question we got wrong. On Wednesday we argued that margin compression was becoming the defining feature of this cycle, on the evidence of Cisco, Coherent and Cerebras. Applied then expanded Semiconductor Systems operating margin by nearly five points, guided the October quarter roughly $700 million above consensus, and reduced China to 28% of revenue from 35% while doing it. The margin thesis holds where the customer has negotiating power, which is networking, optics and specialised silicon selling into hyperscalers and model developers. It does not hold at the tool layer, where the buyer's constraint is capacity rather than price. Equipment is the shortest link in the chain and it prices accordingly.

Our take: the more durable observation is that six of these seven companies delivered a better quarter than expected and the stocks did not care. When beats stop working across an entire complex, the marginal buyer has left and what remains is positioning. That is exactly the condition in which a credit event, or merely a credit analyst's spreadsheet, becomes the thing that moves prices. Friday's Broadcom tape was the proof.

## Corporate: Two Take-Privates and a Payments Endgame

Stripe and Advent International are moving toward a deal for PayPal at roughly $60.50 a share, valuing it near $53 billion, with reports on Friday and Saturday indicating an agreement could be reached within weeks after PayPal rejected the July approach. Silver Lake is separately in talks to take Workday private at a $43 billion valuation, a report that sent the stock up 17.8% on Thursday after a halt for volatility and erased its year-to-date loss.

These are the same trade. Public markets have spent eighteen months de-rating anything they believe an AI agent can disintermediate, whether that is seat-based enterprise software or a payments button competing with Apple Pay. PayPal peaked at a $360 billion valuation in 2021 and is being bid at $53 billion. Private capital is now willing to underwrite both on cash conversion rather than narrative. The read-through is that large-cap application software and legacy fintech trading at a discount to their own history because of an agentic-AI story now have a bid underneath them that did not exist a month ago, and that bid is leverage rather than fundamentals. Note the timing: this is happening in the same week the thirty-year cleared at 5.216%. Sponsors are moving now precisely because they expect financing to get more expensive, not less.

## Sector Spotlights

**Energy.** The best week for the sector since October 2022, up 7.3%, with Brent adding roughly 5% to $88.38 and WTI settling $82.40. Two drivers, both supply-side. Treasury Secretary Bessent promised economic measures against Iran that have "never been seen," pairing unprecedented isolation with a continued naval blockade of Iranian ports, and the International Energy Agency forecast the widest global supply deficit in five years for 2026. A 7.3% sector week inside a 0.4% index week is not rotation, it is a single risk being repriced while everything else stands still.

**Semiconductors and memory.** The split that has defined this market held all week. Memory and storage went up while AI infrastructure went down: SanDisk added 7.0% and Seagate 5.1% on Friday against Broadcom at minus 6.6%, Applied Materials at minus 5.4% and Oracle at minus 4.5%. AMD rose 5.1%. SK Hynix's board approved 54.3 trillion won, about $38.2 billion, for two new fabs at Yongin and Cheongju, breaking ground in 2027 for HBM, DRAM and NAND. Sold-out capacity two years forward funded by committed capital is a physical shortage. A financing vehicle carrying $370 billion of senior debt is a balance sheet. The market spent Friday buying the first and selling the second, and we think that is the correct discrimination.

**Optics and light.** The FCC is drafting a rule barring new Chinese optical transceivers from US data centres, with officials targeting publication this year and immediate effect. Zhongji Innolight holds roughly 27% of the global transceiver market and would lose its US business, which routes demand to Coherent and Lumentum at higher cost to buyers including AWS. Beijing has said it will respond to any action causing material harm. This is the most underappreciated supply-chain item on the board. Optics is the one layer of the AI stack where a single policy signature can reassign a quarter of global supply, and Coherent fell 5% this week on a record quarter with datacom up 59%.

**Space and rockets.** Rocket Lab confirmed on Thursday that the Hart-Scott-Rodino waiting period has lapsed on its $8 billion acquisition of Iridium, that the Form S-4 is filed, that FCC applications are in and that its capital strategy is underway. Backlog reached $2.2 billion, up 20.2% sequentially, with 31 new Electron and HASTE agreements plus five Neutron missions signed in a single quarter, more contract volume than all of 2025. A launch company buying an operating satellite constellation with spectrum licences is vertical integration that changes the revenue quality of the whole sector, and the regulatory path is now materially de-risked.

**Drones and rare earths.** Trump signed a proclamation on Thursday imposing 100% tariffs on drones above 25 kilograms, thermal-imaging models, docking stations and critical components, with 25% on smaller units and a 15% rate for the EU, Japan, Korea, Switzerland, Liechtenstein and Taiwan. It takes effect around September 3. Domestic drone names rallied, and the constraint they cannot tariff away is that US manufacturers still source roughly 90% of motor magnets and 99% of battery cells from China. The tariff creates the demand case for domestic rare earth magnet capacity without creating the capacity.

## Asia and Europe

Korea was the week's standout by a distance. The Kospi rose 2.41% on Friday to a record 6,977.34 after touching 7,010.86 intraday, closing an 11.5% week that broke a seven-week losing streak, led by Samsung, SK Hynix and Kioxia. Japan's Nikkei added about 0.6% to 68,713.80 after setting a fresh intraday record during the session, and the Topix also printed a record. Greater China went the other way for a third consecutive session, with the Hang Seng down 1.0% to 25,137.87 and the Shanghai Composite off 0.5% to 3,908.05.

The regional divergence is the same export-control story visible in Applied Materials reducing China to 28% of revenue from 35%. Capital is concentrating in the two economies that manufacture the memory the buildout requires and draining from the one being fenced out of the equipment that makes it. Korea is now the highest-beta expression of the AI cycle in public markets, which cuts both ways: an 11.5% week followed a 4.58% single-day rout ten days earlier.

Europe finished mixed and lower, with the Stoxx 600 off 0.21% near 659, the FTSE 100 down 0.21% and the CAC 40 down 0.16%. The DAX was the exception at plus 0.53%, which repeats a pattern we have flagged for weeks: Germany is the European index most levered to industrial and semiconductor capital spending, and it participates on the days the chip complex works.

## Geopolitics: The Strait Hardened Again

The most consequential development of the weekend is that the diplomatic track collapsed. Foreign Minister Araghchi said no negotiations with Washington are under way, and Iranian officials indicated Tehran will not return to talks until the end of the current US presidential term in 2029. Iran publicly rejected Trump's claim that the Strait would soon come under American control, with the response that it "cannot be seized with a tweet."

The kinetic track escalated in parallel. The UAE blamed Iran for drone strikes on two ADNOC tankers crossing the Strait on Friday evening, the sixteenth such attack on ADNOC shipping, with no casualties reported. Qatar, Kuwait, Egypt and Bahrain all condemned it. On Saturday the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre reported a bulk carrier hit by an unknown projectile. A carrier strike group is en route to replace the USS Lincoln. Transit volumes remain effectively zero against roughly 73 vessels a day in normal conditions.

Our take: for three weeks the market has been selling crude on headlines about procedural progress toward an Oman-brokered arrangement. That trade no longer has a premise. A counterparty that has ruled out negotiation for three years is not a counterparty working toward a deal, and the energy sector's 7.3% week is the first evidence the market has noticed. We would expect the Hormuz risk premium to be rebuilt rather than unwound from here, and the transmission channel into everything else in this note runs through the September inflation print.
