Thursday, August 20, 2026, written at 8:45am Eastern, forty-five minutes before the bell, with claims and the Philadelphia Fed already out and Walmart down 6%. Yesterday this note called the Treasury buyback expansion a real intervention and said it should be read as one rather than as a technical adjustment. It was, and it has already been half unwound. The 30-year is back at 5.254%, six basis points above where it closed on the announcement. What survived overnight was not the rate relief. It was the currency consequence.

## Top Call: The Backstop Held the Bond for a Day and Broke the Dollar for Longer

The 30-year fell nine basis points on Wednesday to 5.196% after the Treasury said it would at least double long-dated buyback operations from $2 billion to a minimum of $4 billion, running September 9 through November 4 in the 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year buckets. This morning the same bond is at 5.254%, so roughly two thirds of that move is already gone. Meanwhile the dollar index broke below 99 to 98.89, a three-month low, gold surged more than 4% intraday Wednesday before easing 0.95% to $4,474.77, and Bitcoin traded through $70,000 for the first time since June 2 and sits at $71,965, up 10.8%, after about $1.4 billion of short positions were liquidated inside an hour. On the same morning, the national debt crossed $40 trillion.

Our take: read those four prices together and the market is not saying thank you, it is repricing the sponsor. A buyback does not retire debt, it swaps the maturity profile, and doing that in size at the long end while the front end already carries the funding burden is a duration transfer from the private sector to the taxpayer. That is precisely the trade the dollar, gold and Bitcoin have been expressing all week, and all three moved harder than the bond did. ING's Padhraic Garvey put the arithmetic bluntly, calling $2 billion of incremental operation size against $40 trillion of debt something close to rearranging deckchairs, and arguing that only fiscal consolidation solves the structural problem. We agree with the arithmetic and we think it understates the signalling. The size is small. The precedent is not. Washington has now shown it will lean on the long end when auctions stop clearing, which is why the cleanest way to own this view is the currency and the hard assets rather than the bond itself.

## Data: Hot Everywhere Except the Consumer

Initial jobless claims came in at 206,000 against 210,000 expected and 209,000 prior. The Philadelphia Fed manufacturing index printed 47.4 against a 25.0 consensus and 41.4 in July, a very large upside surprise and the second consecutive month of accelerating regional manufacturing after Monday's Empire State reading. The Conference Board leading index for July follows at 10:00am.

Our take: this is the part of the morning that quietly does the most damage to the bull case. The July FOMC minutes released yesterday afternoon were more hawkish than the three dissents implied, with many participants saying tightening would likely be necessary if inflation did not decline, and some arguing that financial conditions may not currently be restrictive enough. September hike odds still fell, to 30.6% from 33.3%, because the market treated a document written before negative payrolls as stale. That is a defensible read. But claims at 206,000 and a manufacturing index near 47 are not stale, they are this morning, and they say the economy is not slowing in the places the Fed watches for demand pressure. Add Brent at $93.57 and the ingredients exist for the hawkish bloc to broaden rather than narrow into Jackson Hole. The front end sitting still at 4.18% through all of this is either patient or complacent, and we lean toward complacent.

## Walmart: A Beat That Told Us the Wrong Thing

| Walmart FQ2 | Consensus | Actual | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $186.26B | $187.94B | +5.9%, beat |
| Adjusted EPS | $0.73 | $0.81 | clear beat |
| US comparable sales | +3.7% | +2.6% | slowest in six years |
| Global e-commerce | n/a | +23% to +24% | still the engine |
| FY adjusted EPS | $2.75 to $2.85 | $2.80 to $2.87 | raised |

Walmart beat on both lines and raised full-year guidance, and the stock is down about 6% because the one number that mattered missed. United States comparable sales grew 2.6% against a 3.7% consensus, the slowest rate in six years. Membership fee revenue rose 17% with a record second-quarter net add for Walmart Plus, global advertising revenue climbed 38%, and management attributed roughly 80 basis points of the comp shortfall to lower pharmacy prices following federal drug pricing changes. Chief Executive John Furner pointed to e-commerce as the offset. The third quarter is guided to $0.62 to $0.64.

Our take: strip out the pharmacy effect and the comp is closer to 3.4%, which is respectable but still a deceleration, and the composition is the part that worries us. Walmart has spent two years winning share from higher-income households trading down, and a slowing comp alongside a 38% advertising line and a 17% membership line says the retail media and subscription flywheel is doing more of the work while the underlying basket does less. Management also flagged gasoline prices as a pressure on discretionary spend, which connects this print directly to Brent at $93.57. Put Walmart next to Target's 3.8% comp inflated by a tariff refund, Lowe's cut to the bottom of its range, and Home Depot's reaffirmation, and the read across the four is consistent: the consumer is intact at the top, thinning in the middle, and the retailers are increasingly earning through margin mechanics rather than volume. That is a fine quarter and a worse trajectory.

## Deere: The Cleanest Print of the Morning, and Nobody Is Talking About It

| Deere FQ3 | Consensus | Actual | Read |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | about $10.9B | $12.61B | roughly 17% above |
| EPS | $4.45 | $5.10 | large beat |
| Construction margin | n/a | 12.1% vs 7.7% | the swing factor |
| FY net income | prior range | $4.75B to $5.00B | raised |

Deere reported net income of $1.379 billion and $5.10 per share against a $4.45 consensus on roughly $12.61 billion of revenue, raised full-year net income guidance to $4.75 billion to $5.00 billion, and called 2026 the bottom of the agricultural equipment cycle. Construction and forestry carried the quarter with operating margin expanding to 12.1% from 7.7%, and small agriculture and turf contributed on higher volumes and price.

Our take: a bottom call from Deere is worth more than a bottom call from almost anyone else, because the company has spent seven quarters working dealer inventory down through the worst farm margin environment in a decade and has guided conservatively the entire way. Construction margin expanding 440 basis points in a year when residential is soft points at infrastructure and data centre site work, which is the same demand that has been showing up in Caterpillar's power generation line. This is a genuine industrial datapoint hiding inside an agricultural report, and it argues the buildout is still pulling through heavy equipment rather than only chips and power. Of the two large prints this morning, this is the more durable signal.

## Asia: Korea Buys Back What It Sold Yesterday

The reversal in Korea was almost as violent as the break. The Kospi closed at 6,852.58, up 5.89%, after opening 3.23% higher and tripping a buy-side sidecar at 9:57am, one session after a sell-side sidecar halted program orders on a 5.80% decline. SK Hynix rose 12.73% to 1,691,000 won and Samsung Electronics rose 9.49% to 271,000 won, on the same 40 trillion won buyback and cancellation and the same commitment to return at least half of cumulative free cash flow that the market sold on Wednesday. Japan's Nikkei added 791 points, or 1.21%, to 66,118 with the Topix up 0.8% to 4,045, and the 10-year Japanese government bond eased back from its three-decade high. Hong Kong's Hang Seng rose 0.80% to 25,698 on health care and technology, the Shanghai Composite added 0.24% to 3,903.72 and the CSI 300 managed 0.09%. The People's Bank of China left the loan prime rates unchanged at 3.00% and 3.50%.

Our take: yesterday this note argued that SK Hynix falling almost 10% on the largest treasury share cancellation in Korean history was a positioning verdict rather than a fundamental one, and that the announcement would read differently once forced selling cleared. We said three weeks. It took one session. That is faster than we expected, and while it validates the mechanism it also makes the volatility itself the story. A market that can move a semiconductor complex of this size 12% in either direction on no change in information is a market whose marginal holder has no anchor, and that condition does not resolve simply because the tape went the right way today. Note also what Asia did not do. The mainland barely participated, with the CSI 300 up nine basis points after Wednesday's 7% semiconductor rout. This rebound is a Korea and Japan event, which is to say a memory and financing event, not a broad Asian risk recovery.

## Alibaba: The Chinese Version of the Same Capex Question

| Alibaba June quarter | Result | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | RMB 268.95B (+9%) | in line with the recovery |
| Net income | RMB 10.54B (-76%) | the cost of the buildout |
| AI cloud and compute | RMB 48.44B (+45%) | the acceleration is real |
| AI product revenue | RMB 12.38B | twelfth straight triple-digit quarter |
| Capital expenditure | RMB 67.68B (+75%) | why profit collapsed |

Alibaba grew revenue 9% to RMB 268.95 billion for the June quarter while net income fell 76% to RMB 10.54 billion, roughly $1.55 billion, as capital expenditure rose 75% to RMB 67.68 billion. The AI cloud and compute segment grew 45% to RMB 48.44 billion and AI product revenue reached RMB 12.38 billion, extending twelve consecutive quarters of triple-digit growth. Adjusted earnings per American depositary share of 8.52 yuan missed a 10.53 yuan consensus and the ADRs are down about 2.6%.

Our take: this is the mirror image of the Wall Street Journal commitment count that helped break the tape on Tuesday, and it is more honest in one important respect. Alibaba pays for its buildout through the income statement in real time, so the cost shows up immediately as a 76% earnings decline rather than sitting in the footnotes as a lease that has not commenced. Tencent did the same thing last week with capex up 190% and free cash flow at negative RMB 13.8 billion. Chinese hyperscalers are running the identical trade as their American counterparts with less financing engineering and worse optics, and investors are marking them down for the transparency. The 45% cloud growth is the number to hold onto, because it is accelerating and it is the evidence that the spending converts into revenue. Anyone who believes the American AI capex cycle will produce revenue should find this print encouraging and the share price reaction difficult to defend.

## Oil and Iran: A Real Escalation, and This Time the Price Moved

Brent rose 2.13% to $93.57 and WTI 2.67% to $86.64, a fifth consecutive session of gains and a three-week high. The cause is not rhetoric this time. Iran fired two ballistic missiles that splashed down in the Persian Gulf late Tuesday, and the Emirati Defence Ministry assessed that they targeted maritime traffic. The United Arab Emirates responded by suspending all trade, commercial exchange and financial transactions with Iran until further notice, ending more than two months of quiet de-escalation between Abu Dhabi and Tehran. Iran's Foreign Ministry denies launching anything. President Trump then announced what he described as an unprecedented economic pressure campaign, promising the most crushing economic operation ever taken against any country and warning third parties of tremendous economic consequences for assisting Iran. The naval blockade stands and no talks are scheduled.

Our take: yesterday this note said the crude price was not reflecting the change in posture and that a one dollar move did not match the language being used. Today it does, and the difference is that something physical happened. Dubai has been Iran's principal commercial and financial gateway for decades, so a full UAE suspension is a sanctions event with actual plumbing behind it rather than a communique. That is why the market paid this time and did not pay for the previous three weeks of threats. We would now treat the UAE suspension, rather than the strait itself, as the escalation channel to monitor, because it converts a maritime conflict into a financial one and pulls Gulf banking into the dispute. The macro transmission is simple and unpleasant. Brent at $93 lands on a Fed whose minutes already worried about insufficient restriction, and it lands on a consumer whom Walmart just described as slowing partly because of fuel.

## What We Are Actually Watching

Three things.

The first is whether the 30-year can hold 5.25% into next week's supply. The buyback is a bid of at least $4 billion per operation starting September 9, which is three weeks away, and between now and then the market has to clear issuance without it. Wednesday showed the announcement is worth about nine basis points. This morning showed that it decays. If the long end makes a new high above Tuesday's 5.327% print before the first operation runs, the intervention will have been revealed as a headline rather than a floor, and equities have not priced that outcome at a VIX below 16.

The second is the dollar, which we now think is the cleanest expression of the whole setup. At 98.89 it sits at a three-month low, and it got there in a week when the Fed minutes were hawkish, regional manufacturing surprised to the upside twice, and oil rose five sessions running. A currency that falls through good domestic data and a hawkish central bank is telling you the market is discounting the fiscal authority, not the monetary one. Gold near $4,475 and Bitcoin at $71,965 are the same trade in different clothes. Watch whether the dollar holds 98.50, because a break there while the Fed is openly debating a hike would be a genuinely unusual combination.

The third is whether Deere or Walmart is the better description of the economy. Deere says the industrial cycle bottomed and construction margins are expanding. Walmart says the consumer is growing at its slowest pace in six years and gasoline is part of the reason. The Philadelphia Fed at 47.4 sides with Deere. The two can coexist for a while, because infrastructure and data centre spending is contractually committed and does not respond to household budgets, but the historical pattern is that the consumer leads and the capital cycle follows with a lag. We are not there yet. For now we would rather own the part of the tape being paid to build things than the part being paid to sell things to people who are buying fewer of them.

All marks above are pre-market and will move by the open.
