The premise of *Send Help* sounds like a dark fantasy for anyone who has ever spent years watching their work get claimed by someone less competent than them: a meticulously capable woman, denied a promotion she was explicitly promised, ends up stranded on a deserted island after a plane crash with the exact boss who wronged her. Suddenly, none of his authority means anything. Every credential, every title, every conference room dominance he accumulated dissolves the moment he needs someone to build a fire.

The closest comparison is *Triangle of Sadness*, and it is not an accidental one. Both films understand that power is not intrinsic, it is environmental. Strip a man of the context that confers his status and you are left with whatever he actually is. In Bradley's case, that turns out to be not very much. He cannot fish. He cannot build shelter. He cannot find water. What he can do is continue to speak to Linda as though she remains beneath him, which is the film's sharpest and most sustained joke, because at this point she is the only thing keeping him alive.

Linda, played by Rachel McAdams in what is genuinely the most interesting work of her career, is not simply a victim transformed into a hero. She is something more complicated and more honest: a person who has been accumulating grievances for years and finally finds herself in the one setting where her specific skill set constitutes total power. Whereas Linda was a mousy wallflower in the civilized world, she blossoms on the island to the point where she looks like she is starring in an ad for a luxury vacation. The transformation is physically visible, and McAdams commits to every stage of it without losing the character's underlying precision. Linda is a strategist. She was always a strategist. The island just finally gives her a board where the game is played on her terms.

What makes the film genuinely engaging rather than merely satisfying is the moment you start to believe Bradley has changed. He shows warmth. He shows humility. He even shows what appears to be genuine gratitude. The film earns the doubt. For a while I really believed him. The revelation that he was poisoning her, trying to engineer his own exit the entire time, lands not with shock but with something colder, the recognition that some people do not change, they simply become temporarily dependent. The arrogance was never gone. It was waiting.

Linda's decision to stay on the island longer than necessary is the film's most psychologically interesting move. This is not a woman desperate for rescue. This is a woman who for the first time in her professional life is watching her boss feel what she felt, incompetent, invisible, entirely without leverage. She gives him shelter, food, water, not out of pure compassion but because she wants him present enough to experience his own diminishment. Every act of care is also an extension of the punishment. The film understands this without over-explaining it.

It is a slow and meticulous power struggle where both characters contend with their darkest attributes. The final confrontation at the mansion, where Linda deploys the strategic thinking that defined her entire career against the man who never valued it, pays off everything the film patiently assembled. She wins not through strength or luck but through preparation, which is exactly how she always worked, and exactly what was never once acknowledged.

One year later, Linda has been rescued and is a wealthy celebrity after turning her survival story into a lucrative self-help career, the coda as wickedly funny as it is thematically complete. The woman who was overlooked is now inescapable. The world rewarded her story the way it never rewarded her work.

That's the joke. That's also the point.
